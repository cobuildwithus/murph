A parallel-bar dip asks you to support your full body weight, lower between the bars, and press back to locked elbows. It demands chest and triceps strength plus a shoulder position that feels stable under load. Build it through assisted dips and general pressing rather than repeatedly sinking into a range you cannot control.

Bench dips are not a required step and can feel uncomfortable because the hands are fixed behind the body. An assisted-dip machine, resistance band, or foot-supported parallel-bar setup more closely matches the final skill and lets assistance decrease gradually.

## What to do

- Train the dip pattern twice per week with at least two days between hard sessions.
- Use assistance that allows 4 to 8 controlled repetitions.
- Start from stable support, lower only as far as the shoulders remain comfortable, and press without bouncing.
- Build general pressing strength with push-ups, bench press, or a machine press.
- Add modest triceps work and plenty of rows or pulldowns.
- Increase range before removing large amounts of assistance.

There is no universal depth. Upper-arm parallel to the floor is a useful reference, but anatomy, bar width, and shoulder history matter. The goal is a repeatable dip through a comfortable range, not forcing the shoulders far below the bars for credibility.

## A simple plan

Twice per week, perform three sets of 4 to 8 assisted dips. Stop with about two good repetitions available. Follow with two sets of push-ups or a chest press, two sets of rows, and two sets of triceps extensions.

When all dip sets reach eight repetitions at the same depth, reduce assistance by one small step. If using foot support, place only enough weight through the feet to keep the movement smooth and gradually use less. A band can work, but record which band and how it is attached.

Once you can complete several repetitions with low assistance, practice a single unassisted lowering: begin at the top, take three seconds to descend to the chosen depth, place the feet down, and reset. Try a full unassisted dip every two weeks when rested.

Keep the entry and exit part of practice. Step or jump to the support position only when the setup allows a controlled start, and use a box or platform to return safely to the floor. On rings, the stability demand is much higher; build a strong ring support and assisted ring dip separately rather than assuming parallel-bar strength transfers immediately.

If the goal is a straight-bar dip for a muscle-up, include that specific bar position after a general base is built. It has a different balance and torso path from parallel bars.

## How to know it is working

Track assistance, depth, and repetitions. Less machine counterweight, a lighter band, less foot pressure, a longer controlled lowering, or a stronger support hold are all progress. Keep bar width and range similar when comparing sessions.

Shoulder comfort matters alongside performance. A plan that reduces assistance while steadily increasing front-of-shoulder pain is not working well. Use a range that can expand gradually rather than treating discomfort as a mobility challenge.

## If you get stuck

If support at the top feels unstable, practice short support holds and build shoulder-blade control with rows and carries. If the bottom is weak, use assisted repetitions with a brief pause at a comfortable depth. If lockout fails, add focused triceps work.

Review total pressing volume. Bench press, overhead press, push-ups, and dips compete for recovery. Put dips first on one day if they are the priority and reduce redundant pressing. If the bar width or fixed hand position remains uncomfortable, another pressing goal may fit your shoulders better.

## A quick note

Use secure bars and a setup that allows a safe step down. Stop for sudden shoulder or chest injury, a new deformity, or marked weakness. People with a history of shoulder instability should be especially conservative with depth and may benefit from individual guidance.
