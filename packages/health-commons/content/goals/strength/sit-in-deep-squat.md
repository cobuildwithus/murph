A comfortable deep squat combines ankle range, hip range, balance, and familiarity with the position. Stance width, toe angle, and body proportions change what the squat looks like. The goal is a stable personal position, not forcing every body into the same foot placement or perfectly upright torso.

Use support early. Holding a door frame, counterweight, or sturdy post lets you explore depth without falling backward. A small heel lift can reveal whether ankle range is a major limiter. These tools help identify the next step; they are not failures.

## What to do

- Practice the position three to five days per week in short, relaxed exposures.
- Experiment with a slightly wider stance and comfortable toe angle.
- Use hand support or a light counterweight to stay balanced.
- Work on ankle dorsiflexion and hip rotation only where they actually limit the movement.
- Strengthen squats and split squats through the deepest comfortable range you control.
- Practice standing out of the position so mobility is paired with strength.

Long passive holds are not the only route. Resistance training through full ranges can improve mobility, and controlled goblet squats can build range, balance, and strength together. Use a mix rather than treating the position as a pure stretching test.

## A simple plan

Four days per week, hold a sturdy support and descend into a squat with the heels down if possible. Spend three rounds of 20 to 30 seconds gently shifting side to side and breathing. Use enough hand support that the position feels exploratory rather than threatening.

Then perform two sets of six goblet or bodyweight squats to a box at your current depth and two sets of eight ankle rocks per side. Twice per week, add split squats and calf raises. If the heels lift, try a thin heel wedge during the squat while ankle range is trained separately.

Every two weeks, reduce hand support, lower the box, use a smaller heel lift, or add ten seconds to the hold. Change one variable. The final goal may use a stance that looks different from a barbell squat, which is fine.

Separate “resting in the position” from “lifting from the position.” The resting squat uses a relaxed, sustainable shape. A loaded squat requires bracing and control. Practice both if both matter, but do not assume a long passive hold automatically makes a heavy squat safer. After a relaxed hold, perform a few controlled rises with support so the body learns to create force from the new depth.

If the goal relates to gardening, play, or floor work, rehearse reaching or shifting within the supported squat. That transfer makes the range more valuable than an isolated photo.

## How to know it is working

Track heel support, hand support, stance, hold time, and comfort. Progress can mean reaching the same depth with less assistance, keeping the heels down, breathing more easily, or standing without using the hands.

Use a side photo monthly if helpful, but do not interpret torso angle as a moral score. Femur length, ankle range, and stance all affect it. The meaningful outcome is a position that feels stable and useful.

## If you get stuck

If you fall backward, use a counterweight and train ankle range. If the hips pinch, adjust stance and toe angle or stop above the pinching point. If the knees feel overloaded, use more hand support, a higher box, and progressive leg strength.

Some bony anatomy limits certain squat shapes. A wider stance or heel lift may be the durable solution rather than a temporary compromise. Do not force a narrow, toes-forward squat because a generic mobility standard demands it.

## A quick note

Use a stable support and avoid dropping into the bottom. Stop for sharp hip or knee pain, new swelling, locking, or repeated giving way. The ability to sit in a deep squat is optional and does not diagnose joint health.
