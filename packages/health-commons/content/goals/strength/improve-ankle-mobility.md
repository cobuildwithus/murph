Ankle mobility usually refers to dorsiflexion: the shin moving forward over the foot while the heel stays down. That range contributes to squats, stairs, walking, running, and landings. The restriction may come from calf tissues, the joint, previous injury, swelling, or simply unfamiliarity with the position.

Measure the movement that matters before stretching everything. The knee-to-wall test is a simple option: keep the heel down and move the knee toward a wall over the middle toes. It is not a diagnosis, but it creates a repeatable starting point.

## What to do

- Practice ankle range three to five days per week in short sessions.
- Use both a straight-knee calf stretch and a bent-knee version.
- Add loaded ankle movement through split squats, calf raises, or controlled step-downs.
- Keep the heel planted and the foot stable rather than collapsing inward to create fake range.
- Include strength for both raising the heel and lifting the front of the foot.
- Practice the target task—squat, stair, or run drill—after mobility work.

Stretching can increase range of motion, and resistance training through range can as well. A plan that combines both is practical. Aggressive banded joint mobilizations are not required, especially when a simple calf stretch and loaded movement create progress.

## A simple plan

Four days per week, do two rounds per side. Hold a straight-knee calf stretch for 30 seconds, then a bent-knee calf stretch for 30 seconds. Follow with eight slow knee-over-toe rocks and eight calf raises through the fullest comfortable range.

Twice per week, add two sets of split squats or step-downs, allowing the knee to travel forward while the whole foot stays planted. Use hand support and a small range at first. After the mobility work, practice five controlled repetitions of the squat, stair, or other movement you want to improve.

Every two weeks, add a small amount of range or load. If a heel wedge immediately improves the target squat, it can be used during strength training while ankle mobility is developed separately.

Include the foot and calf in the plan rather than treating the ankle as a passive hinge. Slow calf raises, controlled lowering from a step, and lifting the toes toward the shin build strength around the available range. For walking or running, gradually expose the ankle to the relevant speed and surface after basic range improves. A large change in a test does not automatically prepare the tissues for a sudden jump in miles, hills, or court time.

If shoes with a raised heel are normally used for the target activity, test in those shoes as well as barefoot. Both results can be useful, but they answer different questions.

## How to know it is working

Use the knee-to-wall test with the same foot position and measure toe distance from the wall. Note whether the heel stays down and the knee tracks over the foot. Test every two to four weeks after the same brief warm-up.

Also track transfer. A better test number matters less if the squat, stairs, or gait remains unchanged. Look for easier depth, a smoother step-down, or less early heel rise during the real task.

## If you get stuck

If range improves during the session but disappears, add strength and task practice within the new range. If the front of the ankle feels blocked or pinched, forcing harder into it may not help; adjust the angle and consider an assessment. If one side remains very different after a prior sprain, strength, balance, and joint-specific rehabilitation may be needed.

Foot position can disguise the limitation. Retest without the arch collapsing or the foot turning far outward. At the same time, do not demand perfect symmetry; small differences are common and may not matter when function is good.

## A quick note

Stop for sharp pain, new swelling, inability to bear weight, or a recent injury that has not been assessed. Calf swelling, warmth, and unexplained shortness of breath require urgent medical attention rather than stretching.
