Knee pain is a location, not one diagnosis. Pain at the front of the knee, an irritated tendon, osteoarthritis, and a recent ligament injury can feel similar while needing different details. Still, many gradual, non-traumatic knee problems improve through the same broad strategy: keep moving at a tolerable level, temporarily adjust the activities that provoke symptoms, strengthen the knee and hip, and rebuild the exact function you want.

Start with a practical outcome. That might be walking for 30 minutes, using stairs, getting out of a chair, gardening, squatting, running, or playing a sport. The goal is not merely to make a pain number smaller. It is to help the knee do more with less disruption and recover more predictably afterward.

## What to do

- Keep some comfortable activity in the week. Complete rest can reduce fitness and confidence without preparing the knee for normal demands.
- Adjust the dose of the clearest aggravating activity. Shorten a run, use a shallower squat, take fewer flights of stairs at once, or reduce jumping volume rather than abandoning all movement.
- Strengthen the knee and surrounding muscles two or three times per week. Useful options include sit-to-stands, squats to a chair, step-ups, split squats, leg presses, hip hinges or bridges, and calf raises.
- Practice the task you want to regain at an easier starting level. Strength work supports function, but walking, stairs, running, and jumping also improve through graded practice of those activities.
- Progress one variable at a time: repetitions, range of motion, resistance, speed, or weekly duration. This makes the knee's response easier to interpret.
- Use a low-impact aerobic option such as walking, cycling, or swimming when it helps you stay active while a higher-impact task is being rebuilt.

Exercise and education are central treatments for common patellofemoral pain and knee osteoarthritis. A therapist may also use taping, a short-term foot orthosis, movement coaching, or manual therapy for a particular presentation. These can support activity, but they should usually help you build capacity rather than become permanent requirements.

## A simple plan

For the first two weeks, choose a starting dose that feels almost conservative. On most days, walk or cycle for 10 to 20 minutes, or split that into shorter bouts. If one activity clearly causes a lasting flare, reduce its duration, frequency, or range by roughly 20 to 30 percent while keeping other tolerable activity.

Two or three nonconsecutive days per week, try this basic session:

1. Sit-to-stand or squat to a chair: 2 sets of 6 to 10.
2. Low step-up or supported short-range split squat: 2 sets of 6 to 10 per side.
3. Hip bridge or light hip hinge: 2 sets of 8 to 12.
4. Calf raise: 2 sets of 10 to 15.

Move through a range you can control. Mild discomfort during exercise can be acceptable when it stays manageable, does not change your movement dramatically, and settles toward your usual level by the next day. If pain, swelling, or limping is clearly worse the following day, reduce the next session's range, repetitions, load, or total activity. If the response is stable for a week, add one or two repetitions, a little range, or a small amount of resistance—not all three at once.

After two weeks, make the plan more specific. Someone aiming for easier stairs can gradually raise the step height and practice controlled step-downs. A runner can rebuild total running time with walk-run intervals before adding hills or speed. Someone with pain during deep squats can expand squat depth gradually while continuing broader leg strength.

## How to know it is working

Review progress once per week rather than judging the plan from one good or bad day. Record one symptom measure and one function measure. For example, rate how much pain interfered with the week, then note walking minutes, comfortable stair flights, chair repetitions, squat depth, or running time.

Progress can look like doing the same task with less pain, doing more before symptoms begin, recovering faster afterward, or feeling less hesitant. Strength and function often improve before pain disappears completely. A useful trend over four to eight weeks matters more than daily noise.

Also notice swelling and the next-day response. Increasing swelling, repeated buckling, or a steadily shrinking activity range suggests that the plan or the assumed problem needs another look.

## If you get stuck

First, check the total load, not just the exercises. A sudden increase in running, court sports, hills, lifting, work demands, or weekend activity can exceed what the knee has recently practiced. Reduce the biggest spike, then rebuild it gradually.

Next, match the exercise to the symptom pattern. Front-of-knee pain may respond well to combined knee- and hip-focused strengthening. A painful tendon may need slower, progressively heavier loading. Osteoarthritis care usually combines local strength work, aerobic activity, and long-term consistency. Recurrent swelling, catching, instability, or pain after a clear injury warrants a more specific assessment rather than endless exercise swapping.

If there is no meaningful improvement after six to eight weeks of consistent, appropriately dosed work, a physical therapist or clinician can examine the knee, clarify the likely source, and tailor progression. Earlier help is reasonable when the diagnosis is uncertain, pain is significantly limiting sleep or daily function, or you cannot find any tolerable starting point.

## A quick note

Seek prompt medical assessment after major trauma, if you cannot bear weight, if the knee truly locks, or if it repeatedly gives way. Urgent care is appropriate for a hot, very swollen knee with fever, rapidly increasing swelling, calf swelling or warmth, shortness of breath, or new severe weakness or numbness. Otherwise, a gradual plan that keeps function in view is usually a sensible place to begin.
