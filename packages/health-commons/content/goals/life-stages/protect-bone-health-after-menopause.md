Bone loss accelerates around the menopause transition, but the goal is not to chase a single density number. The practical outcome is **lower fracture risk and enough strength and balance to keep living normally**. Resistance training, appropriate weight-bearing or impact activity, adequate calcium, vitamin D and protein, not smoking, and treatment when indicated work together.

## What to do

- **Lift weights at least twice per week.** Train the legs, hips, back, chest, shoulders, and arms with progressively challenging resistance. Stronger muscles also support balance and reduce the chance that a stumble becomes a fall.
- **Include weight-bearing movement.** Brisk walking, stairs, dancing, hiking, racquet sports, and similar activities load the skeleton more than cycling or swimming. Those lower-impact activities still help heart and muscle health and can remain part of the plan.
- **Add impact if it is appropriate for you.** Small hops, jumps, or jogging can provide a stronger bone stimulus for some people. Prior fractures, severe osteoporosis, pain, balance problems, and long periods without impact change what is safe.
- **Train balance.** Single-leg work near support, tai chi, step patterns, and changing direction help reduce fall risk. Bone protection includes preventing the fall as well as strengthening bone.
- **Meet calcium needs primarily through food.** Dairy, fortified alternatives, calcium-set tofu, canned fish with bones, and some greens contribute. More calcium is not automatically better, and supplement dose should fill a genuine gap.
- **Get enough vitamin D and protein.** Vitamin D needs depend on diet, sun exposure, absorption, and health history. Protein and enough overall energy support muscle and bone. Avoid chronic under-eating.
- **Do not smoke and keep alcohol modest.** Both are relevant to bone and fall risk.
- **Know when medication matters.** Exercise and food are foundational, but they do not replace osteoporosis medication for someone at high fracture risk.

## A simple plan

For 12 weeks, schedule two full-body resistance sessions and three weight-bearing activity sessions each week. Add five minutes of balance work three days per week. If you are already trained and have no fracture concerns, include a small, progressive dose of impact twice weekly. If not, begin with brisk walking, stairs, and resistance work.

For one week, check whether ordinary meals cover calcium-rich foods and protein. Correct a clear gap with food or a clinician-recommended supplement rather than stacking products. Confirm whether fracture-risk assessment or bone-density testing is appropriate for your age and history, especially after a fragility fracture or with long-term steroid use.

## How to know it is working

Short-term progress appears in stronger lifts, easier stairs and carries, better balance, and fewer near-falls. Bone density changes slowly and may be modest even when fracture protection improves. If you have osteoporosis treatment, use the follow-up schedule chosen by your clinician rather than repeating scans too frequently.

## If you get stuck

Walking alone may not provide enough strength or bone stimulus. Add progressive resistance. If fear of fracture prevents all loading, seek an osteoporosis-informed physical therapist who can select safe movements. If food intake is adequate but bone loss continues, check medical drivers and medication options rather than escalating calcium indefinitely.

Back pain does not automatically mean a fracture, but sudden severe spine pain, height loss, or pain after a minor fall deserves assessment. A fall-prevention plan may also need vision, footwear, home hazards, blood-pressure symptoms, and medicines reviewed.

Build loading around current capacity. Someone new to exercise might begin with sit-to-stands, step-ups, calf raises, rows, and brisk walking; someone already trained may need heavier resistance and safe impact to create a meaningful stimulus. Progress one variable at a time and keep enough challenge that the final repetitions require effort with good form. Swimming and cycling support fitness but provide less skeletal loading, so add weight-bearing and resistance work when appropriate. If fracture risk is high or vertebral fractures are present, avoid copying generic jumping or repeated loaded-spine-flexion routines. A clinician or physical therapist can adapt the same goals without making movement needlessly fragile.

## A quick note

A fracture from a standing-height fall is a major signal even if a prior scan was not labeled osteoporosis. Seek cause-specific assessment and treatment rather than relying on supplements alone.
