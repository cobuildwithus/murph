“Use less” works best when it becomes a specific behavior rather than a vague promise. You might want more cannabis-free days, fewer sessions, less spending, no use before work, or no use in situations where impairment creates risk. Choose the outcome that matters most and measure it simply.

Cannabis products differ greatly in THC concentration, route, and duration. A plan based only on product names or number of puffs can give false precision. Track the pattern you can observe, and involve a clinician when dependence, withdrawal, mental health symptoms, pregnancy, or other substances make the situation more complex.

## What to do

- **Record one typical week.** Note days used, approximate start time, route, labeled THC information when available, setting, reason, and any alcohol or other drug use. Also record whether you drove or did safety-sensitive work afterward.
- **Set one primary limit.** Examples include a fixed number of cannabis-free days, no use before a chosen time, no solitary use, or fewer planned occasions. Avoid changing every variable at once.
- **Protect non-negotiable safety boundaries.** Do not drive, operate machinery, swim alone, supervise children while impaired, or combine cannabis with other impairing substances. Duration is not perfectly predictable, especially with edible products.
- **Reduce access and cues.** Do not carry more than the plan allows. Remove products and equipment from visible, automatic locations and avoid restocking during a craving.
- **Replace the job cannabis was doing.** If it marks the end of work, helps with sleep, reduces boredom, or softens social discomfort, schedule a concrete alternative for that moment.
- **Use support if control is difficult.** Motivational enhancement, cognitive behavioral approaches, and contingency management are among the behavioral approaches used for cannabis use disorder. A clinician or substance-use counselor can help match support to your situation.
- **Plan for withdrawal.** After frequent use, reducing can bring irritability, anxiety, sleep disturbance, appetite changes, restlessness, or cravings. Anticipating them makes the plan easier to sustain.

## A simple plan

Track seven days without changing the pattern. Then choose a two-week target with one number—for example, cannabis-free days per week—and one safety rule. Tell one trusted person what you are changing.

For each usual use period, write the trigger and a replacement. A bedtime cue may need a wind-down routine; an after-work cue may need food, movement, and a transition; a social cue may need a different venue. Put the alternative in the calendar before the craving appears.

Make access fit the target. Avoid bulk purchases, automatic delivery, and keeping products beside the places where you relax. On cannabis-free days, plan the first two hours of the usual use window and leave the setting if the cue becomes strong.

Review after one and two weeks. Count cannabis-free days and unplanned use occasions. Note sleep, mood, concentration, spending, relationships, and next-day function. Keep a limit that is helping, or revise it with support. If repeated attempts do not hold, consider whether abstinence or structured treatment would be a clearer next step.

## How to know it is working

The strongest signals are behavioral: more planned cannabis-free time, fewer unplanned sessions, consistent protection of driving and work, and less time spent obtaining or recovering from use. Functional improvement may include clearer mornings, better follow-through, lower spending, fewer conflicts, or more confidence handling a familiar cue.

Do not use a single mood or sleep score as the verdict. Sleep can temporarily worsen during reduction, especially after frequent use. Review the pattern over several weeks and distinguish short-term adjustment from the longer-term outcome you wanted.

## If you get stuck

If the same setting repeatedly breaks the plan, change the setting, access, or people involved rather than adding another reminder. If sleep is the main barrier, use a direct sleep plan and ask a clinician about persistent insomnia. If anxiety, trauma, pain, or depression drives use, seek care for that problem rather than expecting cannabis reduction to solve it alone.

There is no FDA-approved medication specifically for cannabis use disorder, but evidence-based behavioral treatment and recovery support are available. If you use cannabis with alcohol, sedatives, or other drugs, tell the clinician; combined impairment and withdrawal risks can change the plan.

## A quick note

Avoid cannabis during pregnancy and breastfeeding. Adolescents should avoid cannabis; frequent or high-THC use is especially concerning for the developing brain, and age-appropriate support can help with reduction or stopping. Hallucinations, severe paranoia, inability to care for yourself, suicidal thoughts, chest pain, or repeated vomiting with dehydration need urgent medical assessment. If stopping brings severe symptoms or you cannot reduce despite harm, contact a clinician or local substance-use service.
