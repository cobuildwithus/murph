Loneliness is the feeling that your relationships aren’t giving you the connection you want. Being alone is different: someone can have many contacts and feel lonely, or spend a lot of time alone and feel content. So the first step is to identify which kind of connection is missing, not to meet as many people as possible.

Social connection is linked with important health and well-being outcomes, but that’s no promise that socializing will fix every symptom. Treat connection as a part of health you can practice, and get direct help for depression, anxiety, grief, hearing problems, mobility limits, or other barriers when you need it.

## What to do

- **Name the missing connection.** Companionship, emotional closeness, a group identity, practical support, affection, or just someone to share ordinary time with.
- **Choose repeated settings.** A weekly class, volunteer shift, faith community, walking group, hobby night, sports league, or neighborhood routine creates more chances for familiarity than one-off events.
- **Restart one existing tie.** A short message to a former coworker, relative, neighbor, or friend is often easier than finding a whole new circle.
- **Make small bids for connection.** Ask a follow-up question, stay ten minutes after an activity, suggest coffee, or share a little more honestly than usual.
- **Put contact on the calendar.** A recurring call, meal, or activity keeps connection from waiting until everyone is free.
- **Balance online and in-person contact.** Digital contact can be meaningful, especially with distance or disability. Notice whether a given form leaves you feeling connected or only more aware of other people’s lives.
- **Contribute as well as receive.** Offering a ride, checking in, remembering a detail, or helping with a task builds belonging without turning every interaction into a test.

## A simple plan

Start with a two-week connection map. Write three columns: people you already know, recurring places you could go, and the kind of connection each might provide. Circle one existing relationship and one repeated setting.

Send one low-pressure message today: “I’ve been thinking about you. Want to catch up next week?” Include a specific option so the other person doesn’t have to invent the plan. Then schedule two visits to the repeated setting and go to both, even if the first feels awkward.

During the week, make one small bid each day: a voice note, a sincere question, an invitation, or a five-minute conversation with someone you see regularly. Aim for contact that feels real, not a high message count.

At the end of each week, rate loneliness from 0 to 10 and write one sentence about when it was strongest. Also record meaningful contacts and whether you felt known, useful, or included. Use the pattern to choose the next step: more frequency, a different community, or more depth with one person.

## How to know it is working

Progress may show up before loneliness disappears. You may have someone you can text, a place where people recognize you, one plan to look forward to, or more confidence making an invitation. The central signal is whether the kind of connection you want is becoming more available, not the number of contacts.

Review weekly, because daily loneliness moves with mood and circumstance. Look for less intense lonely periods, quicker recovery after them, more meaningful contact, and a growing sense that reaching out is possible.

## If you get stuck

If outreach keeps going unanswered, widen the pool and use structured settings where attendance is expected. If groups feel overwhelming, start with one-to-one contact, volunteering with a defined task, or an online community with clear norms and recurring sessions.

If you assume people don’t want to hear from you, test that with small invitations rather than treating it as fact. At the same time, respect boundaries and don’t chase one unavailable relationship. Grief, discrimination, relocation, caregiving, disability, and social anxiety can all call for a more tailored plan; community organizations or a therapist may help remove those barriers.

## A quick note

Persistent loneliness can go along with depression and other health problems. If it’s causing a major loss of function, ask a clinician or counselor for support. If you are in immediate danger or thinking about harming yourself, contact emergency or crisis services now; in the United States and Canada, call or text 988.
