A mile swim is an endurance and efficiency goal. In a 25-yard pool, a swimming mile is commonly treated as 1,650 yards; a statute mile is 1,760 yards, and an open-water event may define its course differently. Decide which version you mean, then build continuous distance without letting technique and breathing unravel.

You do not need to swim the full distance every week. Accumulated repeats, one growing continuous swim, and a relaxed pace will prepare you more reliably.

## What to do

- Swim two or three times per week, ideally with at least occasional coaching feedback.
- Make most lengths easy enough that exhalation and stroke rhythm stay calm.
- Build total session distance first, then extend the longest continuous portion.
- Practice the exact pool length or open-water conditions relevant to the goal.
- For open water, add sighting, starts, turns, temperature, visibility, and group skills under supervision.
- Keep shoulders healthy with gradual volume and basic upper-back and rotator-cuff strength.
- Learn the event's exact distance, equipment, and cutoff rules.

## A simple plan

Begin when you can swim 200 to 400 yards or meters continuously. Use two main sessions. In the first, accumulate distance through repeats such as eight 100s with 20 to 40 seconds rest, then add one or two repeats over time. In the second, warm up and complete one continuous easy swim starting around half your current maximum.

Add 100 to 200 yards or meters to the continuous swim after successful weeks. Every third or fourth week, hold or reduce the distance. Keep a few shorter technique repeats in every session so the long swim does not become survival practice.

As you approach the goal, complete sessions totaling more than a mile through repeats and build the continuous swim to roughly 1,200 to 1,500 yards or meters. A full practice mile is useful but not mandatory. If the attempt is open water, complete supervised course-like swims with the wetsuit or equipment you will actually use.

On attempt day, begin slower than your short-repeat pace, settle the breathing first, and divide the distance mentally into quarters.

## How to know it is working

Continuous distance rises without a dramatic increase in stroke rate, breathlessness, or shoulder discomfort. Repeat times become more even, and you recover more quickly after a longer block. You should know the pace you can sustain without relying on an early surge.

Pool and open-water times are not directly comparable. Walls, currents, navigation, drafting, temperature, and measured course length all matter. Completion under the intended conditions is the main outcome.

## If you get stuck

If breathing ends the swim early, slow down and check that you are exhaling underwater. If shoulders end it, reduce pulling volume, improve body position and catch mechanics, and obtain coaching feedback. If you can complete the distance in pieces but not continuously, lengthen repeats and shorten rest gradually rather than making repeated full-distance attempts.

Anxiety in open water needs supervised acclimation, not forced exposure. Begin close to shore with a group and a clear exit.

## A quick note

Do not swim alone in open water or rely on a tow float as rescue. Stop for chest pain, faintness, severe unusual breathlessness, confusion, or loss of safe stroke control.
