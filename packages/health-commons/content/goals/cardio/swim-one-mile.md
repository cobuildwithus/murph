A mile swim is an endurance and efficiency goal. In a 25-yard pool, a swimming mile is commonly treated as 1,650 yards; a statute mile is 1,760 yards, and an open-water event may define its course differently. Decide which version you mean, then build continuous distance without letting technique and breathing unravel.

You don't need the full distance every week; accumulated repeats, one growing continuous swim, and a relaxed pace prepare you more reliably.

## What to do

- Swim two or three times a week, ideally with occasional coaching feedback.
- Make most lengths easy enough that exhalation and stroke rhythm stay calm.
- Build total session distance first, then extend the longest continuous portion.
- Practice in the exact pool length or open-water conditions relevant to the goal.
- For open water, add sighting, starts, turns, temperature, visibility, and group skills under supervision.
- Keep shoulders healthy with gradual volume and basic upper-back and rotator-cuff strength.
- Learn the event's exact distance, equipment, and cutoff rules.

## A simple plan

Start when you can swim 200 to 400 yards or meters continuously, and use two main sessions. In the first, accumulate distance through repeats such as eight 100s with 20 to 40 seconds rest, adding a repeat or two over time. In the second, warm up and swim one continuous easy effort from about half your current maximum.

Add 100 to 200 yards or meters to the continuous swim after good weeks. Every third or fourth week, hold or reduce it. Keep a few short technique repeats in every session so the long swim doesn't become survival practice.

Near the goal, total more than a mile per session through repeats and build the continuous swim to roughly 1,200 to 1,500 yards or meters. A full practice mile is useful but not required. If the attempt is in open water, do supervised course-like swims with the wetsuit or equipment you'll actually use.

On attempt day, start slower than your short-repeat pace, settle the breathing first, and split the distance mentally into quarters.

## How to know it is working

Continuous distance rises without a big jump in stroke rate, breathlessness, or shoulder discomfort. Repeat times get more even, and you recover faster after a longer block.

Pool and open-water times aren't directly comparable: walls, currents, navigation, drafting, temperature, and measured course length all matter.

## If you get stuck

If breathing ends the swim early, slow down and check that you're exhaling underwater. If shoulders end it, reduce pulling volume, improve body position and catch mechanics, and get coaching feedback. If you can cover the distance in pieces but not continuously, lengthen repeats and shorten rest gradually rather than repeating full-distance attempts.

Open-water anxiety needs supervised acclimation, not forced exposure: start close to shore with a group and a clear exit.

## A quick note

Don't swim alone in open water or rely on a tow float as rescue. Stop for chest pain, faintness, severe unusual breathlessness, confusion, or loss of safe stroke control.
