To run a mile without stopping, train the ability to move easily for longer—not the ability to suffer through one desperate mile. Most beginners get there by practicing two or three times per week, using run-walk intervals, and slowing down enough that breathing stays controlled.

A mile is a distance milestone, not a pace requirement. Your first continuous mile may be slower than a brisk walk on hills, and it still counts.

## What to do

- Run on a flat, familiar route or treadmill so pace changes are easy to control.
- Start each session with five to ten minutes of walking.
- Use running intervals that end before you are exhausted and walking intervals that let breathing settle.
- Keep the first half of every session deliberately easy.
- Run on nonconsecutive days until your legs are accustomed to the impact.
- Add simple calf raises, sit-to-stands, and step-ups twice per week.
- Save speed training for after the continuous mile is comfortable and repeatable.

## A simple plan

If you can currently run for one or two minutes, begin with eight rounds of two minutes easy running and one to two minutes walking. Repeat that workout twice in a week, plus one easy walk or cross-training session.

On the third running day—or the next week if you need more recovery—try four rounds of four minutes running and two minutes walking. From there, build the longest continuous segment: six minutes, eight minutes, ten minutes, then 12 to 15 minutes. Keep the total running time similar while joining smaller intervals together.

Once you can run continuously for 12 to 15 minutes with controlled breathing, choose a measured mile. Start slower than your interval pace and avoid surging early. If you need a walk break, take it, finish the distance, and return to training. One unsuccessful attempt is information, not a reason to test again the next day.

Most people should hold each step for at least two successful sessions. If your current continuous run is already ten minutes, you may need only a few weeks; if you are starting from very little activity, give the process longer.

## How to know it is working

Track your longest continuous easy run and the effort at the end. The trend is positive when the running segments grow while breathing, form, and next-day recovery remain stable. A lower effort on the same run-walk session is also progress.

After you complete the mile, repeat an easy mile on two or three separate days before making pace the next goal. The ability is more useful when it is dependable.

## If you get stuck

If you repeatedly stop because of breathing, your running pace is probably too fast. Use shorter steps, relax the shoulders, and accept a pace that feels unusually slow. If leg discomfort stops you first, reduce the running minutes by 20 to 30 percent and use cycling or walking to maintain aerobic work.

If the distance feels psychologically large, train by time and hide the distance display. A continuous 15- to 20-minute run will often bring the mile with it. If you miss a week, resume from the last comfortable interval rather than from the planned calendar date.

## A quick note

Do not run through pain that changes your gait, swelling, or steadily worsening focal pain. Stop and seek care for chest pain, fainting, or severe unusual breathlessness.
