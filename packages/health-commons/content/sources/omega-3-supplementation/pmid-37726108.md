This source is included for **mood_cognition**.

**Findings:** Each 1 g/day n-3 supplementation significantly improved depressive symptoms in adults with and without depression, with larger improvement among those with existing depression; dose-response in existing depression appeared U-shaped with greatest improvement around 1.5 g/day. It did not reduce risk of developing depression in the general population.

**Why it matters:** Supports tracking depressive symptoms as an exploratory endpoint, not claiming prevention.

**Potential experiment signals:** depressive symptoms, remission, incident depression.

**Protocol takeaway:** Dose-response depression review found severity benefit but not prevention benefit. Best signal was in existing depression; general population prevention was not supported.

**Claim use:** `context-only`.

## Extraction details

- **Population:** Adults with and without depression across 67 randomized trials
- **Intervention/exposure:** n-3 fatty acid supplementation at varying daily doses
- **Comparator/control:** Placebo or control arms in RCTs
- **Duration/follow-up:** Varied across trials
- **Endpoints:** depressive symptoms, remission, incident depression
- **Effect estimate or direction:** Each 1 g/day n-3 supplementation significantly improved depressive symptoms in adults with and without depression, with larger improvement among those with existing depression; dose-response in existing depression appeared U-shaped with greatest improvement around 1.5 g/day. It did not reduce risk of developing depression in the general population.
- **Adverse events / safety notes:** Safety outcomes were included in the review title, but detailed adverse-event estimates were not extracted here.
- **Population mismatch:** Treatment effects in existing depression are adjacent clinical evidence; general-population prevention was null.
- **Artifact rights status:** unknown

## Limitations

- Dose-response findings are review-level and should not override protocol dosing safety boundaries.
- Clinical depression and general-population prevention are mixed in the evidence base.
