This source is included for **skin-of-color, visible-light pigmentation, melasma, or PIH safety boundary**.

**Findings:** In Fitzpatrick IV-VI skin, both UVA1 and visible light induced pigmentation; visible-light pigmentation was described as darker and more sustained. Type II skin did not show the same pigmentation response in the extracted record.

**Why it matters:** Use to justify pigmentation monitoring and skin-of-color caution, while not assuming all red/NIR devices carry the same visible-light pigmentation profile.

**Potential experiment signals:** melasma/PIH baseline screen, post-exposure darkening log, Fitzpatrick IV-VI caution.

**Protocol takeaway:** Visible light can induce sustained pigmentation in melanocompetent skin. Broad visible light findings should not be treated as direct red/NIR PBM efficacy or harm evidence.

**Claim use:** `safety-only`.

### Extraction notes

- **Population:** Volunteers with Fitzpatrick skin types IV-VI; additional type II testing was described for comparison.
- **Intervention/exposure:** Long-wavelength UVA1 (340-400 nm) and visible light (400-700 nm) exposure on lower back skin.
- **Comparator/control:** UVA1 versus visible light and skin-type comparison.
- **Duration/follow-up:** Pigmentation followed over two weeks after exposure.
- **Endpoints:** Visual pigmentation, digital photography, diffuse reflectance spectroscopy, confocal microscopy, and biopsy-related pigmentation assessment.
- **Adverse events/safety notes:** Pigmentary change is the key safety signal; this was an exposure study rather than a therapeutic red/NIR protocol.
- **Limitations:** Visible-light broad-spectrum exposure, not isolated red or NIR PBM.; Acute experimental exposure on non-facial skin.; Small physiology study.
- **Population mismatch/directness:** Adjacent pigmentation safety context; not direct protocol evidence for red/NIR skin texture treatment.
- **Artifact/rights status:** paywalled.
