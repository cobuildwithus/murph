This source is included for **guidelines_external_protocol_context**.

**Findings:** The review frames 10,000 steps/day as a physical-activity target, notes that routine daily activity may leave an added-step deficit, and flags pedometer, obsession, and excessive-activity concerns.

**Why it matters:** Useful for 10,000-step history and safety/measurement caveats, not as direct efficacy evidence.

**Potential experiment signals:** daily_step_count, step_goal_attainment, step_count_measurement_error, safety_boundary.

**Protocol takeaway:** Use as context-only history and caveat source; do not treat the review as proving a Daily Step Floor effect.

**Claim use:** `context-only`.

## Extraction notes

- **Population:** Literature review of step-goal, pedometer, physical-activity, exercise, walking, sports, and home-activity contexts; no single participant cohort.
- **Exposure/intervention:** Daily 10,000-step goal, pedometer step counting, walking and other activities to add steps.
- **Comparator/control:** No comparator or control group; narrative review.
- **Duration/follow-up:** Literature searched across MEDLINE 1982-2006 and Google in the accessible abstract.
- **Endpoints:** daily steps; physical activity; pedometer limitations; safety/behavioural issues; activity additions needed to reach 10,000 steps
- **Effect estimates or direction:** Review suggests 10,000 steps can be a target for desirable activity, while routine daily activity may leave a deficit of about 4,000 steps, often 3,000-6,000, that may require more vigorous or added activities.
- **Adverse events/safety notes:** Raises issues that pedometers do not exactly measure energy expenditure, counting steps could foster an obsessive attitude, and excessive walking or physical activity may cause health problems.
- **Limitations:** Narrative review; no pooled effect estimate; older evidence base; not a direct intervention trial.
- **Population mismatch:** Historical 10,000-step target context, not direct evidence for Murph Daily Step Floor outcomes.
