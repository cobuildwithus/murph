## Findings
- GERD symptoms were reported in 30.52% of post-dinner walkers versus 35.17% of sitters and 42.08% of those with post-dinner recumbency. [source_artifact:pmid-22204202]
- Walking versus lying was reported with OR 0.66 (95% CI 0.50 to 0.88). [source_artifact:pmid-22204202]
- Longer dinner-to-bed intervals were associated with lower GERD symptom prevalence. [source_artifact:pmid-22204202]

## Why it matters
This source is directly named around post-dinner walking but should be used as observational context rather than proof that walking treats GERD. [source_artifact:pmid-22204202]

`context-only`.

## Extraction details
- **Population:** Multiethnic South Asian adult outpatient-clinic relatives without major comorbidities, smoking, or alcohol use, surveyed for GERD symptoms.
- **Intervention or exposure:** Habitual regular post-dinner walking and longer dinner-to-bed interval as lifestyle exposures.
- **Comparator or control:** Post-dinner sitting or recumbency and shorter dinner-to-bed intervals.
- **Duration or follow-up:** Cross-sectional survey conducted from February 2009 to March 2010; no prospective intervention follow-up.
- **Endpoints:** GERD symptom prevalence; post-dinner activity; dinner-to-bed interval
- **Effect estimates or direction:** GERD symptoms were reported in 30.52% of post-dinner walkers versus 35.17% of sitters and 42.08% of those with post-dinner recumbency. Walking versus lying was reported with OR 0.66 (95% CI 0.50 to 0.88). Longer dinner-to-bed intervals were associated with lower GERD symptom prevalence.
- **Adverse events or safety notes:** No walking-related adverse events were extracted; the study design was observational.
- **Population mismatch:** Population and exposure are observational post-dinner habits, not a controlled every-meal walking protocol.
- **Directness to Walking After Every Meal:** observational_context
- **Claim-use boundary:** context-only
- **Artifact rights status:** unknown

## Limitations
- Cross-sectional observational design cannot establish causality.
- Exposures and symptoms were questionnaire-based.
- Sample was a selected clinic-relative population rather than a randomized community sample.
- Walking dose, pace, and timing were not experimentally standardized.
