This source is included for **adjacent-timing-exercise-variants**.

**Findings:** The report describes 30 minutes of walking as soon as possible after lunch and dinner compared with waiting 1 hour, with weight loss in two individuals over about one month. The design lacks randomization, adequate sample size, and controlled comparator structure for protocol efficacy claims.

**Why it matters:** It is often cited in consumer claims about walking immediately after meals, so it should be preserved as context but not used as efficacy evidence.

**Potential experiment signals:** Weight change, Timing after meals, Self-reported discomfort, Consumer-claim boundary.

**Protocol takeaway:** Do not use this source as evidence that the protocol causes weight loss; at most, it is context for why immediate-after-meal timing claims exist.

**Claim use:** `context-only`.

## Extraction details

- **Population:** Two Japanese adults described in a preliminary case-report/self-experiment context.
- **Intervention/exposure:** brisk or strolling walking immediately after meals
- **Comparator/control:** Delayed 1-hour post-meal walking period in a preliminary report.
- **Duration/follow-up:** Exploratory walking timing comparisons and a one-month immediate-after-meal walking period after lunch and dinner.
- **Endpoints:** Weight change, Timing after meals, Self-reported discomfort, Consumer-claim boundary
- **Adverse events/safety notes:** The authors reported no abdomen pain, fatigue, or discomfort in the two described people, but this is not robust safety evidence.
- **Limitations:** Two-person preliminary report; self-experiment/case-report design; weight-loss endpoint; no adequate control; not glycemic-outcome evidence.
- **Population mismatch:** Direct timing topic but not valid for general adult efficacy claims or glycemic protocol claims.
