This source is included for **Medication, pregnancy, liver disease, and mental-health safety boundaries (part 2)**.

## Quick read

- **Source type:** Systematic review and meta-analysis (2025).
- **People studied or addressed:** Patients with alcohol-associated liver disease receiving AUD treatment in liver-disease or transplant contexts.
- **Role in Murph:** safety-boundary evidence only.
- **Most relevant Murph signals:** liver disease status, AUD treatment engagement, alcohol relapse or abstinence, liver-related outcomes, mortality in transplant contexts.

## Findings

- **intervention_result** — The meta-analysis included 25 eligible studies with 93,899 patients, including 33,834 receiving an AUD intervention. Outside the liver-transplant setting, pooled data from 7 RCTs found AUD treatment reduced alcohol relapse by 73% (HR 0.27, 95% CI 0.15 to 0.46), and medication trials reduced relapse by 77% in 5 RCTs with 322 patients (HR 0.23, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.39). These are clinical liver-disease treatment findings, not direct wellness-challenge evidence.
- **intervention_result** — In liver-transplant recipient analyses, observational studies suggested a mortality reduction with AUD treatment, but this was not confirmed in two RCTs (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.38 to 1.79). This mixed clinical evidence should be preserved as liver-disease treatment context rather than promoted into a direct short-term abstinence-challenge claim.

## Why it matters

Reinforces that alcohol-associated liver disease is a clinical-care boundary for AUD treatment and abstinence goals, not a routine wellness challenge population.

## Protocol takeaway

Use as a liver-disease boundary: users with known or suspected alcohol-associated liver disease need clinical AUD/liver-care pathways rather than a self-guided challenge framing.

## Claim use

`safety-only`.

## Important limits

Patients with alcohol-associated liver disease, including transplant contexts; not general short-term abstinence challenge users. The liver-disease and transplant treatment setting is a major population mismatch; RCT evidence remains limited for some outcomes and settings.
