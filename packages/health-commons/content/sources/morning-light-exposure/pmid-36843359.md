**Findings:** Fifty-six typically developing adolescents (29 girls, 27 boys; mean age 13.59 years) completed six nights at home with actigraphy, sleep logs, light-exposure logs, and questionnaires. Longer self-reported morning daylight/outdoor light was associated with longer objectively measured sleep duration. Longer evening electronic-device use was associated with later objective sleep onset/offset, shorter duration, and greater day-to-day variability. Self-reported light exposure was not associated with sleep satisfaction, alertness/sleepiness, or sleep efficiency in accessible summaries.

**Why it matters:** Addresses morning daylight/outdoor timing in free-living sleep, but the adolescent and self-report exposure context is an adjacent variant.

**Potential experiment signals:** objective sleep duration, sleep onset time, sleep offset time, sleep efficiency, sleep satisfaction, alertness, evening device use

**Protocol takeaway:** Morning daylight/outdoor light was associated with longer objective sleep duration in adolescents, but several subjective outcomes were null and population mismatch is substantial.

**Claim use:** `context-only`.
