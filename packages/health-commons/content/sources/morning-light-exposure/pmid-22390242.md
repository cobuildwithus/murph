This source is included for **free_living_observational_measurement**.

**Findings:** In 88 student workers, evening types reported lower subjective sleep quality, higher chronic work-related fatigue, and less regular social rhythms than other chronotypes. Objective sleep parameters did not differ between chronotype groups. Evening types had lower waking-hours light exposure 2-11 hours after wake time and lower exposure in the 100-500 lux range; bright-light exposure at or above 1000 lux did not differ.

**Why it matters:** It preserves mixed evidence: chronotype groups differed in subjective outcomes and moderate light exposure, but not in objective sleep parameters.

**Potential experiment signals:** waking-hours light exposure, chronotype, subjective sleep quality, work-related fatigue, sleep efficiency.

**Population mismatch / directness:** Young student workers, not general adult protocol users; exposure was observed, not assigned. Directness: `adjacent_variant`.

**Limitations:** young adult student-worker population; observational group comparison; chronotype confounding; not outdoor-specific; no intervention.

**Protocol takeaway:** Use as context for chronotype-related light patterns and fatigue, not as proof that morning light improves sleep.

**Claim use:** `context-only`.
