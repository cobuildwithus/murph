This source is included for **Melanopic/evening-light mechanism**.

**Findings:**

- In everyday-life data from UK adults, higher pre-bedtime light exposure was associated with longer sleep-onset latency; this supports the timing rationale but remains observational and noncausal.

- The study demonstrates a feasible approach to collecting longitudinal melanopic light exposure and sleepiness/sleep-timing data in everyday life, relevant to future Murph experiment signals.

**Why it matters:** Gives real-world adult context for pre-bedtime light as a sleep-onset-latency signal and highlights melanopic light measurement as an experiment endpoint.

**Potential experiment signals:** pre-bedtime light exposure, sleep-onset latency, sleep timing, sleepiness, melanopic EDI

**Protocol takeaway:** Use as observational same-mechanism support; do not infer that glasses caused shorter sleep-onset latency.

**Claim use:** `supports-protocol`.

## Extraction notes

- **Study design:** Longitudinal observational cohort
- **Population or measurement target:** UK adults in everyday life without an externally imposed circadian challenge
- **Intervention/exposure or measurement:** wrist-worn light logging and sleep/sleepiness data collection
- **Comparator/control:** No assigned comparator; observational contrasts across real-world pre-bedtime, daytime, and nighttime light exposure patterns.
- **Duration/follow-up:** Seven-day longitudinal monitoring
- **Directness to Red Light Glasses Before Bed:** `same_mechanism`.
- **Population mismatch:** Everyday-life adults are relevant, but the study did not assign red-light glasses or reduce light experimentally.
- **Safety/adverse-event notes:** No adverse-event finding; observational measurement source.

## Limitations

- Observational design cannot establish causality.
- Light behavior may be confounded by schedule, chronotype, and lifestyle.
- Does not test eyewear adherence or lens dose.
