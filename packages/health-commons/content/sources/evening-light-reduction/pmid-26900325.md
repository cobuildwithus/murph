This source is included for **Adjacent non-eyewear or population-mismatch context**.

**Findings:**

- The review summarizes biological plausibility that blue light can influence circadian physiology and ocular processes, supporting the mechanism for reducing short-wavelength light at night without providing eyewear-specific sleep efficacy.

- The review discusses possible ocular effects of blue light, but it does not establish that consumer blue-blocking or red glasses prevent eye disease or improve bedtime sleep outcomes.

**Why it matters:** Provides mechanism-level plausibility for evening short-wavelength light reduction while also requiring separation from untested eye-protection claims.

**Potential experiment signals:** circadian physiology, melatonin, ocular physiology, retinal safety

**Protocol takeaway:** Use for mechanism and claim boundaries only; cite direct eyewear trials for sleep outcomes.

**Claim use:** `context-only`.

## Extraction notes

- **Study design:** Narrative review
- **Population or measurement target:** General circadian and ocular physiology literature
- **Intervention/exposure or measurement:** blue-light exposure physiology rather than a bedtime-eyewear intervention
- **Comparator/control:** Not applicable; narrative review of mechanisms and ocular physiology.
- **Duration/follow-up:** Not applicable
- **Directness to Red Light Glasses Before Bed:** `same_mechanism`.
- **Population mismatch:** Review literature spans mechanisms and preclinical/experimental contexts rather than adult home red-light-glasses use.
- **Safety/adverse-event notes:** Use as safety context only; do not imply disease prevention from glasses.

## Limitations

- No source-owned sample size or pooled effect estimate.
- Review-level claims are adjacent to, not direct evidence for, the bedtime glasses protocol.
- Ocular hazard discussion should not be converted into a consumer eye-protection claim.
