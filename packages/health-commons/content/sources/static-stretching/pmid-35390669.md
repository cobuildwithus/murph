This source is included for **adjacent_variant_boundaries**.

**Findings:** The review included 32 trials with 1122 participants; 27 trials with 911 participants were meta-analyzed. Eccentric exercise improved joint flexibility or fascicle-length outcomes with a pooled standardized mean difference around 0.54 (95% CI 0.34 to 0.74).

**Why it matters:** Shows that eccentric exercise is a plausible alternative flexibility-training pathway, creating a boundary around claims that at-home static stretching is uniquely necessary for flexibility.

**Potential experiment signals:** joint range of motion, muscle fascicle length, loaded-mobility or eccentric-training sibling signal.

**Protocol takeaway:** Use as context only: eccentric training can improve flexibility, but it is not an at-home static-stretching protocol.

**Limitations and mismatch:** Adjacent modality, variable eccentric-training programs, moderate heterogeneity, and evidence quality ranging from low to high. No direct at-home static-stretching dose or adherence inference should be made. Population mismatch: Intervention is eccentric exercise rather than static stretching; populations and muscles vary across trials.

**Claim use:** `context-only`. Context-only boundary for future eccentric/loaded-mobility sibling protocols.
