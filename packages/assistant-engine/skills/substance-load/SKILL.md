# Substance Load

Owns substance-load reasoning for common user questions about caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, cannabis, and adjacent substances that affect sleep, recovery, energy, and training.

Use this as Murph operating guidance, not as a consumer article. Ground the answer in the current conversation, vault context, and wearable data before recommending. Ask at most one missing question when the answer would materially change the next step. Keep clinician, safety, and existing skill handoffs intact.

## Research Guidance

Skill 4: substance-load

Operating manual for Murph when caffeine, alcohol, or nicotine may be driving sleep disruption, elevated overnight heart rate, lower HRV, poor recovery, anxiety-like arousal, or next-day fatigue.

1. Scope and ownership

This skill owns caffeine, alcohol, and nicotine as lifestyle levers that affect sleep timing, sleep continuity, sleep architecture, overnight RHR/HRV, recovery scores, and next-day energy. It should reason from the user's own logs, wearable patterns, and conversation history before recommending a change.

This skill does not own addiction medicine, diagnosis of substance use disorders, alcohol withdrawal management, smoking cessation pharmacotherapy, prescription medication changes, sleep apnea diagnosis, insomnia CBT-I, jet lag or shift-work protocols, or broad fatigue workups.

Route boundaries:

User pattern	Primary owner
"How much caffeine is too late?" "Is alcohol tanking my HRV?" "Do nicotine pouches affect sleep?"	substance-load
Sleep hygiene, insomnia behavior plan, sleep restriction, sleep anxiety	sleep-improvement
Light timing, jet lag, social jet lag, shift work, delayed sleep phase	circadian-rhythm
Persistent fatigue after substance pattern is addressed, anemia/thyroid/depression/OSA workup	energy-fatigue
Alcohol dependence, withdrawal, relapse prevention, nicotine cessation meds, varenicline/bupropion/NRT dosing	clinician/addiction medicine
Chest pain, fainting, severe palpitations, seizures, confusion, alcohol poisoning, severe withdrawal	urgent care/emergency

Core stance: substance-load is not a morality skill. It should frame caffeine, alcohol, and nicotine as variables that can be tested against the user's own data.

Sleep onset problem: "What's the latest caffeine or nicotine you usually have?"

Waking at 2-4 a.m.: "Any alcohol or nicotine in the evening?"

Daytime fatigue with heavy caffeine: "How much caffeine total, and when's the first and last dose?"

3. Lever -> outcome map with evidence tiers

Evidence tier definitions:

Tier	Meaning
Strong	Meta-analysis, clinical guideline, or repeated controlled human studies
Moderate	Good controlled studies or large observational/wearable datasets, but not definitive
Weak/mechanistic	Plausible physiology, limited direct outcome evidence
Popular but unsupported	Common advice with little evidence or wrong causal framing
Caffeine

Important physiology: caffeine is mainly metabolized by CYP1A2; average half-life is about 5 hours, but reported adult ranges are roughly 1.5-9.5 hours, with slower clearance in pregnancy and with some medications/oral contraceptives, and faster clearance in smokers. That variability is why a "2 p.m. cutoff" works for some people and fails for others.
NCBI
+1

Controlled sleep data are strong enough to give practical timing guidance. The Drake JCSM study found that 400 mg caffeine taken at bedtime, 3 hours before bed, or 6 hours before bed significantly disrupted sleep, supporting at least a 6-hour cutoff for substantial doses.
PMC
 A 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis found caffeine reduced total sleep time by about 45 minutes, reduced sleep efficiency by about 7 percentage points, increased sleep latency by about 9 minutes, and reduced deep sleep; its timing guidance estimated that a typical 107 mg coffee should be taken at least 8.8 hours before bed and a 217.5 mg pre-workout at least 13.2 hours before bed to avoid meaningful sleep disruption.
ScienceDirect
 A newer controlled dose/timing trial found 100 mg had no significant objective sleep effect in that study, while 400 mg disrupted sleep even up to 12 hours before bedtime; participants also did not reliably perceive the objective disruption.
OUP Academic

Safety and dose guardrails: FDA consumer guidance says up to 400 mg/day is often cited as an amount not generally associated with dangerous effects for most adults, but sensitivity varies widely; the FDA also warns that rapid consumption around 1,200 mg can produce toxic effects such as seizures.
U.S. Food and Drug Administration

ACOG

Caffeine withdrawal: symptoms can start 12-24 hours after stopping, peak around 20-51 hours, and last 2-9 days; headache is common, and withdrawal can occur even around 100 mg/day in susceptible users.
ResearchGate

Alcohol

Alcohol is often misread because it can make sleep onset feel easier while worsening sleep quality and recovery. A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis found alcohol consistently delayed REM onset and reduced REM duration, with dose-response effects; even low doses up to 0.50 g/kg, roughly around two standard drinks depending on body size, disrupted REM, and higher doses worsened REM more despite sometimes shortening sleep onset.
Monash University
 In U.S. guidance, one standard drink contains 0.6 fl oz / 14 g of pure alcohol; NIAAA defines binge drinking as about 4 drinks for women or 5 for men in about 2 hours, and heavy drinking thresholds include 4+ drinks on a day or 8+ per week for women and 5+ on a day or 15+ per week for men.
NIAAA
+1

Wearable effects are usually obvious. In a large real-world wearable study with more than 20,000 adults and over 5 million person-days, one drink more than a person's own average was associated with roughly +2.4 to +2.8 bpm higher nocturnal resting heart rate and about 3.3-3.8 ms lower HRV, with dose-response effects; earlier drinking, longer sleep, and reduced next-day strain attenuated but did not erase the effect.
PLOS

ABC

Nicotine: cigarettes, vaping, pouches, gum/lozenges, patches

Nicotine is a stimulant and dependence-reinforcing drug. Users often describe it as relaxing because it relieves withdrawal or craving, but physiologically it can increase arousal and fragment sleep.

A Sleep Medicine Reviews review of 52 studies found nicotine consumption was associated with insomnia symptoms, longer sleep latency, more fragmentation, lower slow-wave sleep, lower sleep efficiency, more daytime sleepiness, and often REM suppression.
PubMed
 In a controlled acute transdermal nicotine study, nicotine reduced total sleep time by about 33 minutes, reduced sleep efficiency, reduced REM percentage, and increased initial sleep latency.
Mayo Clinic
 For vaping, systematic-review evidence links e-cigarette use with poorer sleep outcomes, though much of the evidence is observational and confounded by dual use, age, stress, and underlying nicotine dependence.
Frontiers

Nicotine pouches should be treated as nicotine exposure, not as "sleep-neutral because there is no smoke." CDC states nicotine pouches contain nicotine, can contain high nicotine levels, are addictive, are not safe for youth, young adults, or pregnancy, and are not FDA-approved as a quit-smoking aid.
CDC
 CDC also states e-cigarettes are not safe, commonly contain nicotine, and are unsafe during pregnancy.
CDC

4. Wearable-data interpretation

Murph should use wearables for pattern detection, not diagnosis.

What devices measure reasonably well

Consumer devices are generally useful for:

Sleep/wake timing.

Total sleep duration trends.

Overnight resting heart rate trends.

HRV trends relative to the user's own baseline.

Same-user comparisons across tagged nights, especially alcohol vs no alcohol.

A validation study comparing Apple Watch, Garmin, Oura, WHOOP, Polar, and Somfit against polysomnography found sleep/wake agreement around 86-89%, but multi-stage sleep classification was only about 50-65%. That means the model should trust broad sleep duration/timing more than exact "REM minutes" or "deep sleep minutes."
PubMed
 AASM guidance on consumer sleep technology emphasizes that these tools should not be used to diagnose or treat sleep disorders without appropriate clinical evaluation.
WashU Research Profiles

What devices measure poorly or noisily

Do not over-interpret:

One-night "deep sleep" or "REM" changes.

Exact sleep-stage minutes.

Recovery score as a medical measure.

Alcohol, nicotine, or caffeine causality from one night.

HRV comparisons to population norms.

Sleep apnea, insomnia disorder, arrhythmia, or alcohol use disorder diagnosis.

Typical noise bands and trend rules

Use within-person deltas.

Marker	How to use	Practical noise/trend rule
RHR	Strong for alcohol nights and illness/stress	A same-night alcohol rise of ~2-5+ bpm is often meaningful if repeated
HRV	Useful but noisy; compare to personal baseline	Prefer 7-day rolling average or matched-night comparisons; HRV is sensitive to illness, training, stress, menstrual phase, and alcohol
Sleep duration	Useful for caffeine/alcohol experiments	Look for repeated 20-45 min changes, not one-night noise
Sleep latency	Useful for caffeine/nicotine timing	Look for repeated 10-20 min improvements
WASO/awakenings	Useful for alcohol/nicotine	Needs several nights; single-night awakenings are noisy
REM/deep sleep	Directionally useful only over repeated nights	Do not make strong claims from one night's stage score

For HRV, a 7-day rolling average is more useful than reacting to daily values; sports-science HRV guidance commonly uses weekly averages or smallest-worthwhile-change logic rather than single-day interpretation.

Clean self-experiment template

Murph should recommend only one change at a time.

Caffeine experiment:
For 7 nights, keep caffeine before a chosen cutoff. Track caffeine mg/time, bedtime, sleep latency, total sleep, awakenings, and next-day energy. Use a stricter cutoff for higher dose: 8-10 hours before bed as default; 10-12+ hours for 300-400 mg, pre-workout, insomnia, or high sensitivity.

Alcohol experiment:
For 1-2 weeks, tag every drinking night with number of standard drinks and last-drink time. Compare alcohol-free nights with drinking nights, ideally matched for bedtime and training load. Primary outcomes: RHR, HRV, respiratory rate, total sleep, awakenings, next-day energy.

Nicotine experiment:
For 7-14 nights, remove nicotine after dinner or at least in the final 2-4 hours before bed. Track nicotine type, dose/strength, last use, sleep latency, awakenings, and cravings. If reducing/cessation causes withdrawal insomnia, interpret over 3-4 weeks, not the first 2-3 nights.

5. Myths and failure modes
Myths Murph should actively correct
Myth	What Murph should say instead
"Alcohol helps me sleep."	"It may make you fall asleep faster, but it reliably worsens sleep architecture and recovery, especially REM, HRV, and RHR."
"One or two drinks is too little to matter."	"For some people, even one drink shows up in RHR/HRV. The clean test is your own no-drink nights versus drink nights."
"Clear liquor is better for sleep."	"For sleep and recovery, ethanol dose and timing matter more than liquor type."
"Nicotine pouches/vapes don't count."	"They count. The sleep-relevant exposure is nicotine."
"Nicotine relaxes me."	"It can feel calming because it relieves craving, but it is still a stimulant and can fragment sleep."
"I don't feel caffeine at night, so it isn't affecting sleep."	"People can miss objective caffeine sleep disruption; the wearable/sleep log experiment is more reliable than feel alone."
"The wearable says deep sleep was low, so I need a supplement."	"Stage estimates are noisy. First check the high-signal levers: alcohol, late caffeine, nicotine, illness, and schedule."
"Cold turkey is the cleanest caffeine experiment."	"Not if withdrawal creates headaches and fatigue. A taper can be cleaner because it avoids withdrawal noise."
"Hydration cancels alcohol."	"Hydration may help hangover symptoms, but it does not make alcohol REM- or HRV-neutral."
"A hard workout will sweat out alcohol."	"Time clears alcohol. Hard training can add stress to an already stressed recovery system."
Common user failure modes

Changing caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, bedtime, supplements, and exercise all at once.
Murph should narrow to one lever: "Let's isolate one variable for 7 nights so your data can actually answer this."

Chasing single-night recovery scores.
Murph should reframe: "One night is a clue. Three to seven repeated nights make it useful."

Ignoring dose.
"Caffeine after lunch" is too vague. 50 mg tea, 150 mg coffee, and 350 mg pre-workout are different.

Ignoring timing.
"Only two drinks" at 10 p.m. can be worse for sleep than one drink at 6 p.m.

Using wearable sleep stages as proof.
Murph should use RHR/HRV/sleep duration first, stages second.

Quitting caffeine abruptly, then blaming the plan when withdrawal causes fatigue.
Use tapering and explain the 2-9 day withdrawal window.

Trying nicotine cessation as a simple sleep hack.
Full cessation is a health win, but early withdrawal can worsen sleep. Do not overpromise short-term sleep improvement.

Treating alcohol reduction as "all or nothing."
Harm-reduction framing is valid: fewer drinks, earlier drinks, alcohol-free nights, and nonalcoholic substitutions can all be meaningful.

Re-measurement rules Murph should use

Alcohol: same-night wearable effects are meaningful, but call a pattern after at least 3-5 paired nights.

Caffeine: use 7-14 nights, because withdrawal and schedule variability can hide the signal.

Nicotine: use 7-14 nights for evening cutoff; 3-4 weeks for cessation/reduction because withdrawal affects sleep.

Do not compare to generic norms. Compare user to user.

Use the smallest clear win. For example: "Your HRV is 18% higher and RHR 4 bpm lower on alcohol-free nights" is more useful than "your recovery score improved."

Final operating principle: Murph should make substance advice testable, specific, and reversible. The best recommendation is usually not "quit everything"; it is "isolate the highest-likelihood lever for one week, measure the user's own response, then decide."
