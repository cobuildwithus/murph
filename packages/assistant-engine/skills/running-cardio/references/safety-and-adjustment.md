# Safety and adjustment reference

Use this file for screening, symptoms, return after time off, clinical handoffs, and recovery decisions. Apply safety reasoning quietly; do not force every user through a medical questionnaire.

## Scope

Running-cardio may program general exercise for a user who is asymptomatic or has stable, already-understood restrictions that do not require a new clinical decision.

It does not:
- diagnose symptoms
- clear a musculoskeletal injury
- create a post-operative or diagnosis-specific return-to-run protocol
- override clinician instructions
- prescribe through syncope, chest symptoms, severe breathlessness, or post-exertional worsening

## Preparticipation reasoning

Avoid blanket medical-clearance barriers. Consider:
- whether the user is currently active
- known cardiovascular, metabolic, or renal disease when relevant
- current signs or symptoms
- intended exercise intensity
- medication or clinician restrictions

An asymptomatic beginner can usually start with light-to-moderate activity and progress gradually. A symptom, known condition, or planned vigorous jump may change that decision.

When risk is unclear, give a lower-intensity, reversible option while directing the user to appropriate care rather than withholding all movement by default.

## Immediate stop and urgent-care signals

Stop exercise and seek urgent evaluation as appropriate for:
- chest pressure, squeezing, or pain, especially with exertion
- fainting, near-fainting, collapse, or new severe dizziness
- severe or unusual breathlessness that does not settle normally
- new one-sided weakness, facial droop, confusion, or other neurological concern
- palpitations with faintness, chest symptoms, or severe breathlessness
- signs of severe heat illness
- another symptom the user experiences as acutely unsafe

Do not reassure from a normal wearable number.

## Musculoskeletal ownership

Use `physical-therapy` first for:
- new, worsening, focal, or recurrent pain that changes training
- swelling, locking, instability, weakness, or altered gait
- inability to walk or bear weight normally
- suspected stress injury
- post-operative or post-fracture return
- questions about whether an injury is ready for running
- a return plan after a clinician-diagnosed injury

Running-cardio can later program within explicit readiness criteria and restrictions.

Persistent pain as the central problem may instead require `chronic-pain-support`, with running-cardio contributing a tolerable cardiovascular dose.

## Illness and exertion intolerance

Use `chronic-illness-support` or clinical care when capacity is materially shaped by:
- post-exertional symptom exacerbation
- syncope or presyncope
- unexplained tachycardia or marked orthostatic symptoms
- oxygen or respiratory limitations
- active treatment burden
- a significant flare
- clinician-prescribed heart-rate or exertion limits

Do not use generic “push through and adapt” logic for post-exertional worsening.

After an ordinary short viral illness, resume below the prior dose once the user is improving and daily activity is tolerated. Persistent chest symptoms, severe breathlessness, faintness, or a concerning course warrants medical evaluation.

## Ordinary pain-free return after inactivity

Running-cardio may lead when all are true:
- no current pain that changes gait or function
- normal comfortable walking
- no unresolved injury, surgery, fracture, or bone-stress concern
- no return-to-run clearance question
- the break was ordinary inactivity rather than rehabilitation

Useful default:
- start below the former peak
- use two or three easy exposures
- use run-walk when continuous running is uncertain
- separate early impact days
- progress comfortable time or running fraction first
- delay sustained or interval quality until the response is stable

A previous injury history lowers the threshold for conservative dosing and PT handoff if symptoms recur; it does not prove injury is inevitable.

## Response model

### Green response

- intended effort was accurate
- movement stayed controlled
- familiar mild fatigue resolves normally
- daily function is normal
- next-day response is close to baseline

Action: repeat or progress one small lever.

### Yellow response

- easy work became unexpectedly hard
- form or gait became less controlled without acute injury
- soreness or fatigue lasts longer than expected
- sleep, stress, heat, illness, or life load changed capacity
- the user repeatedly dreads or avoids the plan
- a wearable trend conflicts with how the user feels

Action:
- repeat rather than progress
- shorten duration
- reduce running fraction or incline
- remove quality
- add recovery
- substitute a lower-impact modality
- move the session
- review the plan's life fit

Yellow is information, not failure.

### Red response

- acute stop signal above
- rapidly worsening focal pain
- inability to bear weight or walk normally
- major gait change, instability, or new neurological symptom
- symptoms that violate clinician restrictions

Action: stop, route, and do not progress.

## Pain and soreness

Normal exertion can cause familiar diffuse muscle fatigue or delayed soreness. Concerning patterns are more likely when pain is:
- focal
- sharp or escalating
- changing gait or mechanics
- accompanied by swelling, weakness, instability, numbness, or loss of function
- worsening across sessions or at lower loads
- persisting at rest or at night without a familiar explanation

There is no universal safe pain number across diagnoses. When pain changes the plan, physical therapy owns the assessment.

## Recovery inputs

Useful low-burden signals:
- sleep and unusual stress
- session RPE
- intended versus actual effort
- lingering fatigue or soreness
- desire or aversion that is out of character
- performance or form change
- later-day and next-day function

Do not create an elaborate readiness score. A single low wearable score does not automatically cancel training; a normal score does not override symptoms.

## Adjustment hierarchy

When the plan is too hard, change the least disruptive lever first:
1. reduce pace, resistance, or incline
2. shorten the work segment or session
3. lengthen recovery or add walking
4. change modality
5. remove quality
6. reduce frequency
7. change mode or hand off

When the plan is too easy, progress one lever only after confirming it is repeatable and still serves the goal.

## Strength and sport recovery

Count hidden load:
- hard games or practices
- sprints and plyometrics
- heavy lower-body lifting
- metcons and circuits
- physically demanding work
- unusual hiking, travel, or standing

A yellow response may be a scheduling problem rather than a cardio-fitness problem. Move or reduce the conflicting session before adding recovery gadgets or complex metrics.

## Medication and heart-rate limitations

Some medications and conditions alter heart-rate response. Use talk test, perceived effort, symptoms, and clinician guidance rather than forcing generic zones.

Do not change medication timing or dose. Route medication-specific questions appropriately.

## Weight-loss and compulsive-exercise guardrails

Pause weight-loss cardio programming and route appropriately when the user describes:
- exercise to compensate for eating
- panic or guilt about missing sessions
- rapid or unexplained weight loss
- severe food restriction
- recurrent dizziness or fainting
- menstrual disruption or other possible low-energy-availability signs
- exercise despite injury or medical advice

Do not reinforce calorie-burning contests, food-earning, punishment, or escalating volume to “make up” for a miss.

## Pregnancy, postpartum, and other special clinical contexts

General movement may be appropriate, but new symptoms, high-risk pregnancy, postpartum return-to-run readiness, pelvic-floor symptoms, surgical recovery, or clinician restrictions require the relevant clinical owner. Do not issue a generic calendar-based clearance.

## Environment and equipment

Adjust for:
- heat, humidity, altitude, cold, darkness, and air quality
- traffic, water safety, and route isolation
- footwear or pack problems
- unfamiliar machines or rowing/swimming technique

Environmental substitution is sound programming. Do not frame indoor or shorter alternatives as lesser moral choices.

## Safety communication

Keep routine safety language proportional. State the specific reason for a handoff, give the safest useful next step, and avoid alarming lists when no relevant signal exists.

For a normal plan, one concise stop/adjustment rule is usually enough. For an actual concern, be explicit.
