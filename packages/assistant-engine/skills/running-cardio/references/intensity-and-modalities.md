# Intensity and modality reference

Use this file for effort prescription, Zone 2 questions, heart-rate interpretation, modality choice, and substitutions.

## Intensity is a domain, not a magic number

External load describes the task:
- pace
- power
- speed
- incline
- resistance
- distance
- duration

Internal load describes the person's response:
- talk test
- perceived effort
- breathing
- symptoms
- local muscular fatigue
- session and next-day response

Use both when available. When they conflict, investigate context instead of assuming the device or the user is wrong.

## Practical intensity language

### Easy aerobic

Typical cues:
- RPE about 2–4/10
- full sentences are comfortable
- breathing is elevated but controlled
- movement can continue for a useful duration
- the user finishes with reserve

Easy does not require a specific pace. Walking may be the correct easy intensity for a beginner, on hills, in heat, or after time off.

### Sustained quality

Typical cues:
- RPE about 6–7/10
- concentration is required
- speech is limited but not impossible
- output is controlled and repeatable
- the session is not a race or maximal test

The exact physiological threshold varies with testing method, duration, modality, and day-to-day state. Prescribe the workout, not a mystical “threshold pace.”

### Interval quality

Typical cues:
- hard aerobic work, often reaching RPE 8–9/10 late in the set
- breathing is high
- output and technique remain broadly stable
- recovery is sufficient to make the next repetition useful

Intervals need not be all-out. Repeated maximal failure usually creates more fatigue and local load than the general cardio goal requires.

### Relaxed speed

Typical cues:
- brief acceleration
- quick but smooth mechanics
- low accumulated fatigue
- full recovery

Do not use relaxed speed as a disguised sprint workout.

## Zone 2 without taxonomy confusion

“Zone 2” can refer to different things:
- the second band on a consumer device's five-zone chart
- the middle zone of a three-zone physiological model
- work immediately below a first lactate or ventilatory threshold
- a colloquial label for easy conversational cardio

These meanings are not interchangeable. In a common three-zone model, work below the first threshold is often called Zone 1; in many five-zone systems, part of that domain is called Zone 2.

When exactness matters, ask which system or test the user is using. Otherwise translate the request into the intended training domain:
- steady work below the first threshold
- full-sentence talk test
- RPE roughly 2–4/10
- heart rate as supporting evidence, not a verdict

A user may need to slow down, walk hills, reduce resistance, or shorten the session to stay easy.

Do not claim:
- one formula accurately finds every person's boundary
- all work must remain in a narrow heart-rate band
- Zone 2 has unique adaptations that no other intensity can produce
- more Zone 2 is always better

## Heart rate

Heart rate can help when:
- the device is reasonably reliable for the modality
- the user knows their tested or observed values
- conditions are comparable
- the number reduces uncertainty rather than increasing obsession

Interpret cautiously when affected by:
- heat and humidity
- dehydration
- altitude
- illness
- caffeine or stimulants
- stress and poor sleep
- cardiac or other medications
- optical-sensor error
- rapid arm movement, gripping, rowing, or swimming
- cardiac drift during long sessions

Formula-derived maximum heart rate and watch zones are population estimates. Treat them as starting clues. Do not force a user to speed up merely because heart rate looks “too low,” or to ignore concerning symptoms because it remains “in zone.”

## Pace and power

Use pace or power when:
- the user has a stable baseline in that modality
- terrain and environment are reasonably comparable
- the workout is specific enough to benefit

Do not carry a running pace directly to hills, heat, trails, treadmills, cycling, rowing, or elliptical work. Use internal effort and modality-specific metrics.

## Session RPE

A simple post-session rating can support adjustment:
- “How hard did the whole session feel, 0–10?”

Duration multiplied by session RPE can summarize training load when a user already tracks it, but it is not an injury predictor or a reason to build a new readiness system.

## Modality selection

Choose the modality that best satisfies the current purpose while fitting preference, access, skill, symptoms, and local load.

| Modality | Useful for | Main cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Walking | Beginners, recovery, health, high repeatability | May need incline or duration for higher stimulus; foot tolerance still matters |
| Incline walking | Higher aerobic demand without running speed | Calf/Achilles demand, treadmill holding, steep-grade form |
| Running | Running-specific economy and impact tolerance; time-efficient load | Higher impact and tissue demand; pace ego can distort easy work |
| Cycling | Low-impact aerobic volume and intervals | Saddle fit, local quadriceps fatigue, traffic or setup |
| Rowing | Whole-body aerobic work and intervals | Technique, back/hip tolerance, local grip/posterior-chain fatigue |
| Elliptical | Low-impact continuous work with simple pacing | Machine-specific feel; less transfer to running impact |
| Swimming | Low-impact conditioning and breathing skill | Technique often limits effort; access and water safety |
| Rucking | Loaded walking, occupational or preference-specific conditioning | External load increases foot, leg, back, and shoulder stress |

No modality is universally best. Enjoyment and low setup friction can outweigh small theoretical advantages.

## Substitution rules

Match a substitute by:
1. purpose
2. internal effort
3. useful duration
4. local tissue demand
5. recovery cost

Examples:
- easy run -> easy bike or elliptical at conversational effort for similar time, adjusted for comfort
- running intervals -> bike or row intervals using the same work/recovery pattern and internal effort
- longer easy run -> longer low-impact session when impact reduction is the main need

Do not claim mile-for-mile, calorie-for-calorie, or exact “equivalent” conversions across modalities.

A substitution preserves the cardiovascular purpose; it may not preserve running economy, impact tolerance, technical skill, or sport specificity.

## Running and run-walk

Run-walk can control intensity and impact while preserving running practice.

Choose work and walk intervals that let the running segments remain easy. Common starting ratios are merely examples; set them from current tolerance. Progress one variable at a time.

Walking breaks do not invalidate an aerobic session. They are often the best tool for full-sentence effort, hills, heat, and early return.

## Incline and hills

Incline raises cardiovascular and local muscular demand. Use effort rather than flat-ground pace.

For hill intervals:
- choose a grade that preserves mechanics
- keep repetitions controlled
- use generous recovery
- account for calf, Achilles, and quadriceps load

Downhill running can add substantial eccentric load even when heart rate is low. Do not treat it as free recovery.

## Rucking

Treat load, distance, speed, terrain, and frequency as separate progression levers.

Good defaults:
- begin with ordinary walking tolerance
- use a well-fitted pack
- keep the first loads modest
- avoid running with load for general fitness
- change one load variable at a time
- monitor feet, shins, knees, hips, back, and shoulders

Rucking can be valuable, but it is not automatically safer or easier than unweighted walking.

## Environmental modifiers

### Heat and humidity

Reduce pace, power, duration, or intensity; use perceived effort and symptoms. Allow acclimatization. Hydration needs are individual and context-specific.

Stop for confusion, collapse, severe weakness, or other heat-illness concern and seek urgent help as appropriate.

### Cold, ice, and darkness

Prioritize traction, visibility, route safety, and warm clothing. Indoor substitution is a complete training decision, not a failure of discipline.

### Altitude

Expect higher breathing and perceived effort at familiar outputs. Let effort lead during acclimatization.

## Wearables

Useful:
- duration
- broad heart-rate trends
- repeatable route or power comparisons
- longitudinal patterns interpreted with context

Less authoritative:
- calorie expenditure
- proprietary readiness or recovery scores
- single-session VO2-max estimates
- auto-detected zones without validation

Numbers are clues. A metric should simplify the decision; otherwise use talk test, effort, symptoms, and function.
