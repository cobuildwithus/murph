# Preparing for an event or benchmark

Read this when a dated race, competition, challenge or qualifying standard, or a named benchmark such as a 5K under 25 minutes, should shape the member's training. It applies at every level, including a first event. For a strength meet such as powerlifting or weightlifting, the lifting program comes from the main training skill and this file adds the rules check, the taper and the day itself.

The aim is that the member starts healthy, is prepared for what the event actually demands, and has a simple plan they can follow under pressure.

## Check the facts first

Rules, movement standards, qualifying times, cutoffs, the course, required equipment, aid stations and wave format change the advice, and they change from year to year. Look up the organizer's or governing body's current published information before relying on any of them. Stop looking once the decision is supported. If you cannot find a rule, say it is unverified. Not finding a rule does not mean there is none.

## Build the plan

1. Decide which goal this is, because it settles the trade-offs.
   - Complete: durability, margin against cutoffs, safe skills, logistics, fueling the member tolerates, and conservative pacing.
   - Perform: training aimed at the member's main limiter, harder benchmarks and more event-specific work.
   - Qualify or place: a verified standard, an honest statement of the gap and the risk, and no promise of the result.
2. Start from what the member has done in the last four to eight weeks: how often, how long, and their longest or most event-like sessions. Do not judge readiness from ambition, age, body size, one wearable score or one heroic session.
3. Choose one main limiter, such as durability, sustainable pace, grip, a skill, transitions, terrain, fueling or pacing, and at most one secondary limiter.
4. Choose the current phase by the job it does.
   - Recover and assess: restore normal function and decide what comes next.
   - Foundation: repeatable frequency, easy volume, basic strength and safe skill practice.
   - Build: improve the main limiter.
   - Specific: rehearse the event's sequence, terrain, equipment, fueling and standards at a controlled cost.
   - Taper: shed fatigue while keeping sharpness and confidence.
5. Protect the two or three sessions a week that address the limiter and let the rest flex around life. For each protected session, give its purpose, the dose, how hard it should feel, and when to stop or change it.

The time left shapes what is possible:

| Time to the event | What it allows |
|---|---|
| Days to about three weeks | Stay healthy, practice logistics and pacing, keep familiar training and taper. Do not chase new fitness. |
| About four to eight weeks | Work on one or two decisive gaps from the current level, with enough event-specific practice to remove surprises. |
| About nine to twenty weeks | Usually room for foundation, build, specific and taper phases in an order that fits the member. |
| More than twenty weeks | Staged goals and general development, with event-specific blocks from time to time. |

A short runway does not speed up adaptation. Reduce the scope, switch the goal to completing, or reconsider the target. Do not pack missed months into a few weeks.

## Rehearsal, fueling and the day

- Rehearse race fueling, drinking and equipment in selected event-specific sessions. Nothing new on the day: no new food, supplement, shoe or pacing plan.
- Use the Health Commons result for carbohydrate and fluid amounts, and turn the plan into things the member has practiced, such as gels, bottles or chews, with times and where they will get them.
- Give a pacing plan by effort, one or two cues to focus on, a short routine for resetting after a mistake, and a fallback plan for a bad patch.
- Introduce open water, technical descents, riding in a pack, obstacles and heavy movement standards step by step and with supervision.
- For a member under 18, protect growth, school and sleep, involve a qualified adult, and never suggest a weight cut, stimulants or supplements.

Missed periods, repeated bone stress injuries, weeks of failing to recover, or signs of disordered eating can mean the member is eating too little for their training. Say so, recommend a sports medicine clinician or dietitian, and reduce the training load while keeping the event goal in view.

To test a fueling, caffeine, equipment or warm-up choice before the event, read `experiment-onboarding`. When the hard part is doing the key sessions week after week, read `behavior-followthrough`.

## After the event

Separate the result from how the member executed. Keep what worked, pick one change to preparation and one change to execution, and then choose what comes next: recover, maintain, rebuild, a new target, or stopping.
