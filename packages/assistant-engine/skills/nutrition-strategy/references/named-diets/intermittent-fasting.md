# Intermittent fasting

Use this progressive-disclosure reference only after the parent `nutrition-strategy` contract selects the named-diet lane. The parent remains authoritative for routing, safety, tracking burden, clinical boundaries, and answer shape. This file distinguishes intermittent-fasting variants and adds diet-specific execution guidance; it is not a registered skill or a therapeutic fasting protocol.

## Decision summary

- Intermittent fasting is a family of timing and intermittent-energy-restriction patterns, not one diet. Time-restricted eating, 16:8, 14:10, 5:2, alternate-day fasting, and one meal a day are not interchangeable.
- In adults studied for overweight, obesity, or cardiometabolic risk, fasting patterns can produce modest short-term changes in weight and surrogate markers, but they are generally not superior to a sustainable non-fasting energy-restriction pattern. Evidence for adverse effects, long-term durability, and direct health outcomes is limited.
- Earlier time-restricted eating has a modest average advantage over late time-restricted eating for some surrogate outcomes, but eating-window duration has not shown a consistent dose-response. A shorter window is not automatically better.
- For an adult without caution flags who wants a simple timing boundary, prefer the least restrictive viable version: first remove unwanted late eating or use a roughly 12-hour overnight gap; if a named fasting pattern is still desired, start with 14:10. Do not jump to 5:2, alternate-day fasting, or OMAD.
- Adequate food, medication safety, eating-disorder recovery, sleep, social life, and training fuel outrank keeping a fasting streak.

## What the label can mean

| Pattern | Common meaning | Material distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **Time-restricted eating (TRE)** | Food and energy-containing drinks are kept within a recurring daily window; total energy is not necessarily prescribed. | The window may be early, middle, late, or self-selected. TRE does not tell Murph whether the person eats for 6, 8, 10, or 12 hours, or whether intake falls. |
| **14:10** | A 14-hour fast and 10-hour eating window each day. | Usually leaves room for three eating occasions and is the least restrictive common named version. It is not a biological threshold. |
| **16:8** | A 16-hour fast and 8-hour eating window each day. | Often compresses intake to two or three occasions. A noon-to-8 p.m. version differs from an 8 a.m.-to-4 p.m. version in sleep, training, family, and circadian fit. |
| **5:2** | Five usual-intake days and two days each week with little or no energy; research often uses modified, not zero-energy, fast days. | It is a weekly intermittent-energy-restriction pattern, not daily TRE. Consecutive and nonconsecutive fast days create different practical burdens. |
| **Alternate-day fasting (ADF)** | Alternating usual-intake days with zero- or very-low-energy days. | Restriction occurs about every other day, so fueling, social, and rebound-eating pressure is usually greater than with 5:2 or moderate TRE. |
| **One meal a day (OMAD)** | One meal in a short daily window, often implying about 22-23 hours without food. | This is a highly compressed daily pattern with direct evidence much thinner than for broader TRE. It makes adequate total intake and distributed protein or training fuel harder. |

A label also may not specify water, energy-free drinks, milk, supplements, or medication timing. Do not turn "clean fasting" rules into a purity test. Prescribed medication, hydration needs, and safe fueling take precedence over preserving a technical fast.

## What the evidence supports

### Comparative efficacy

- The 2026 Cochrane review found that intermittent fasting made little to no difference in weight loss or quality of life compared with regular dietary advice in adults with overweight or obesity. Included trials lasted no longer than 12 months, and adverse-event evidence was uncertain.
- A 2025 network meta-analysis of 99 randomized trials found broadly similar weight and cardiometabolic effects for intermittent fasting and continuous energy restriction. ADF had the only average advantage over continuous restriction for body weight, about 1.3 kg, which was below that review's prespecified threshold for a clinically important difference. Most trials were short, and most participants had an existing health condition.
- A 10-hour TRE trial published in 2026 found short-term reductions in weight and fat in selected adults with obesity. That result shows that a feasible TRE protocol can work for some people; it does not establish superiority, long-term durability, or direct disease prevention.
- A one-year 5:2 trial found similar modest weight outcomes to standard brief dietary advice. Participants initially liked the simple rule, but reported adherence fell substantially over time. Preference can make a pattern useful without making it more efficacious.

### Meal timing versus energy intake

- TRE often reduces eating opportunities and therefore energy intake. Trials that prescribe similar energy intake in both groups generally find little or no added weight-loss advantage from the time window itself.
- Timing may still have independent circadian effects. A 2026 network meta-analysis found early TRE modestly better than late TRE for body weight and fasting insulin, while rankings by eating-window length were inconsistent. These are surrogate outcomes, not proof that an early window prevents disease or extends life.
- A short, intended-isocaloric crossover trial found that early and late 8-hour windows shifted circadian timing but did not produce clinically meaningful cardiometabolic differences. This supports a plausible clock effect while limiting claims about its health importance.
- Do not promise that 16 hours, 20 hours, or any other duration activates a clinically validated "autophagy stage." Cellular mechanisms and ketone production are plausible research pathways, not a consumer clock for guaranteed outcomes.

### What is not established

- No fasting variant has high-certainty randomized evidence for lower all-cause mortality, cardiovascular events, cancer, dementia, or longer human lifespan.
- Evidence is dominated by short- to medium-term trials in adults with overweight, obesity, or metabolic risk. It transfers poorly to pregnancy, breastfeeding, adolescence, frail older adults, people with low intake, eating-disorder recovery, lean athletes, or high-volume training.
- Short-term weight or biomarker changes do not establish long-term adherence, diet quality, psychological safety, or net benefit for a specific person.

## Why users may choose it

Common reasons include fewer food decisions, a clear boundary around late-night eating, dislike of calorie tracking, a schedule that naturally favors later or earlier meals, appetite preference, weight or biomarker goals, religious or cultural familiarity with fasting, and claims about metabolism, longevity, or "discipline." Clarify the actual job before recommending a protocol.

Treat a preference for simple rules as useful fit information. Do not treat hunger tolerance, longer fasts, or a tighter window as virtue. Religious fasting may have distinct food, fluid, timing, and community rules; do not silently replace it with a research TRE protocol.

## Lowest-risk practical execution

1. **Confirm the goal and owner.** A definition or ordinary meal-timing question can stay here. Intentional fat loss, muscle gain, calorie targets, or a plateau belongs with `body-composition`; marker, condition, or medication goals belong with clinical owners.
2. **Start wider than the user's maximum tolerance.** First try a consistent overnight gap near 12 hours or a 14:10 pattern. Tighten only when the person is eating enough, feels well, and can name a benefit that the tighter window adds.
3. **Fit the window around real life.** Protect sleep opportunity, work, family meals, culture, and the most demanding training. If early and late windows fit equally well, an earlier finish is a reasonable preference; do not force an early window that causes missed recovery food, very early waking, or social isolation.
4. **Protect meal quality and distribution.** Keep roughly three meaningful protein-containing eating occasions when feasible. Build meals from flexible options: eggs, yogurt, milk, tofu, beans, fish, poultry, meat, or fortified alternatives; oats, bread, rice, potatoes, pasta, tortillas, fruit, or other demand-matched carbohydrate; vegetables or another fiber-rich food; and nuts, seeds, avocado, olive oil, cheese, sauce, or another satisfying fat or flavor.
5. **Keep an easy fallback.** Examples include yogurt with fruit and cereal; a bean-and-rice bowl; eggs or tofu with toast and fruit; a sandwich with milk or a fortified alternative; or a frozen meal plus produce. Convenience food is compatible with a useful eating window.
6. **Return to the next normal meal after a deviation.** Do not compensate for a late meal, social event, or large meal by extending the next fast.

### Variant-specific guidance

- **14:10:** Preferred first named version. It often preserves breakfast, lunch, and dinner or three shifted eating occasions without requiring a separate diet.
- **16:8:** Use only when an 8-hour window still permits adequate meals and training fuel. If intake becomes rushed, the last meal becomes very large, or protein is compressed into one or two weak occasions, widen to 14:10.
- **5:2:** Not a default first step. Do not invent a fast-day calorie target or rigid menu in this reference. If an appropriate user has already chosen it, favor nonconsecutive lower-demand days, ordinary balanced eating on the other days, and no "earning" or repayment cycle.
- **ADF:** Do not present its small average short-term weight advantage as a reason to accept greater burden. Frequent low-intake days commonly collide with training, social meals, and adequate intake; 14:10 or a regular daily pattern is the preferred off-ramp.
- **OMAD:** Do not recommend as a routine starting pattern. One meal makes total energy, protein distribution, carbohydrate around exercise, fiber, micronutrients, and medication-with-food needs harder to meet and can promote an overly large meal or rebound eating. Offer two or three eating occasions within a wider window instead.

## Exercise, protein, and under-fueling

- Fasted exercise is optional, not a marker of fitness and not clearly superior for performance or fat loss. A short easy session may be fine when the person feels normal; this does not justify applying the same approach to long, hard, high-volume, or repeated sessions.
- Place the eating window so a demanding session can receive familiar carbohydrate beforehand and carbohydrate plus protein afterward. A banana with yogurt, toast with eggs, cereal with milk, a sandwich, or rice with tofu or poultry are options. Breaking the fast is the correct repair when fuel is needed.
- Daily adequacy and distributed protein matter more than defending a narrow window. If a strength, muscle, or recovery goal cannot fit the parent's protein distribution guidance, widen the window rather than cramming everything into one meal.
- Watch for persistent fatigue, falling performance, slow recovery, recurrent illness or injury, dizziness, feeling unusually cold, irritability, sleep disruption, menstrual change, reduced libido, food preoccupation, or unexpected weight loss. These are reasons to stop tightening the window and assess under-fueling, not proof of low motivation.

## Common failure modes and repairs

| Failure mode | Lowest-burden repair |
|---|---|
| Skipping food all day, then eating rapidly or beyond comfort at night | Move food earlier, widen to 14:10 or regular meals, and add a planned protein-plus-carbohydrate meal or snack before extreme hunger. |
| Recurrent rebound or loss-of-control eating after fasting | Stop the fasting protocol and restore regular nourishment; route eating-disorder risk rather than adding more rules. |
| A late window worsens sleep, reflux, or family fit | End earlier if feasible, widen the window, or use regular meals. Route persistent digestive symptoms to `gut-digestion`. |
| A strict early window removes post-training food | Move or widen the window, or eat the recovery meal and end the fast. Training recovery outranks timing theory. |
| Protein, produce, or total food is crowded out | Add another eating occasion and use easy foods; do not solve low total intake with protein alone. |
| Excess caffeine is used to suppress hunger | Reduce the fasting demand and eat. Do not use stimulants as a substitute for adequate food or sleep. |
| Weekday rigidity produces weekend compensation | Use a flexible 12-hour or 14:10 baseline instead of all-or-none rules. |
| A missed window is treated as failure | Resume at the next ordinary meal. No punishment, extra fasting, or compensatory exercise. |
| The person tracks every minute without a decision benefit | Drop timer precision. A repeatable meal boundary and a few feedback signals are enough. |

## Monitoring, stop conditions, and off-ramps

Use the lowest useful monitoring burden. A short fit check can ask whether the pattern preserves stable daytime energy, ordinary hunger rather than extremes, sleep, training quality and recovery, social flexibility, and enough food. Do not require calorie logging or daily weighing. Glucose monitoring belongs to an existing clinician-directed plan, not this reference.

**End the fast and follow the person's existing medical plan** for shakiness, sweating, confusion, faintness, or other suspected low-glucose symptoms. Seek urgent help for confusion, fainting, chest pain, severe weakness, inability to keep fluids down, severe dehydration, or symptoms that do not improve.

**Stop the protocol and return to regular nourishment** for recurrent dizziness, headaches or nausea; sleep worsening; persistent fatigue; training decline; menstrual change; food preoccupation; recurrent rebound or loss-of-control eating; substantial unintentional weight loss; or any sign that the window is displacing needed food or medication.

Use the least restrictive off-ramp that resolves the problem:

- 16:8 to 14:10, then to a simple overnight gap or regular meals;
- 5:2 or ADF to a consistent daily pattern;
- OMAD to two meals plus a snack, then to three ordinary eating occasions when useful.

If a tighter pattern offers no clear valued benefit, keeping it is unnecessary. Do not wait for harm or treat a wider window as failure.

## Groups needing extra caution

Do not provide a self-directed fasting protocol when any of these are present:

- pregnancy or breastfeeding;
- adolescence or anyone under 18;
- frailty, sarcopenia, low appetite, low intake, substantial unintentional weight loss, or recovery from prolonged very low intake;
- a known or suspected eating disorder, severe restriction, recurrent loss-of-control eating, purging, compulsive exercise, intense food or weight fear, or number sensitivity;
- current under-fueling or a training load that already exceeds available recovery;
- post-bariatric surgery or another clinician-managed nutrition plan.

Require clinician or registered-dietitian involvement before fasting when diabetes, recurrent hypoglycemia, kidney disease, advanced liver disease, significant heart disease, a relevant endocrine disorder, or a fluid restriction is material. The same applies to insulin or another medicine that can cause hypoglycemia, any medicine that must be taken with food, or a medicine whose safety changes with dehydration or major meal-timing changes. Do not recommend medication adjustments.

Acute illness, surgery or injury recovery, heat exposure, endurance events, and other periods with increased food or fluid needs are reasons to suspend the fasting goal or obtain individualized guidance.
