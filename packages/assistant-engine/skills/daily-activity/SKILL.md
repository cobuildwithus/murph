# Daily Activity

Owns everyday movement outside formal training: steps, NEAT, sedentary breaks, walking, activity snacks, and wearable activity trend interpretation.

Use this as Murph operating guidance, not as a consumer article. Ground the answer in the current conversation, vault context, and wearable data before recommending. Ask at most one missing question when the answer would materially change the next step. Keep clinician, safety, and existing skill handoffs intact.

## Research Guidance

Skill 4: daily-activity

Operating purpose: This skill helps Murph turn steps, sedentary time, walking pace, post-meal movement, and NEAT into one realistic daily behavior change. It is the default movement skill for users who are not already following structured endurance or strength training.

1. Scope and ownership
Owns

This skill owns:

Domain	Murph should handle
Steps/day	Baseline, trend, age-adjusted targets, realistic step-goal progression, interpreting low/high step days.
Sedentary time	Long sitting bouts, "active couch potato" risk, practical sitting breaks, standing versus walking breaks.
NEAT	Incidental movement: errands, chores, stairs, walking calls, active commuting, parking farther away, walking meetings.
Walking-first fitness	Brisk walking, casual walking, pace/cadence, walking as the first exercise prescription for inactive users.
Post-meal walking	Short walks after meals for glucose spikes, post-meal sleepiness, and CGM pattern changes.
Habit formation for activity	Step-goal adherence, self-monitoring, prompts, anchors, weekly review, avoiding overcorrection.
Device interpretation	Consumer step-count accuracy, cross-device changes, phone-versus-watch caveats, score-chasing prevention.
Does not own

Route away when the user's main need is:

Adjacent topic	Route to
VO2 max improvement, Zone 2, intervals, race prep, endurance blocks	aerobic-fitness
Running form, mileage progression, race training, run injury prevention	running / aerobic-fitness
Strength, muscle gain, bone density, balance training, sarcopenia prevention	strength / resistance-training skill
Sleep debt, insomnia, sleep apnea suspicion, recovery scores	sleep / recovery skill
HRV or resting heart rate interpretation	hrv-resting-heart-rate
Weight-loss nutrition, calorie targets, GLP-1 medication questions	nutrition / medical skill
New chest pain, fainting, severe shortness of breath, neurological symptoms	urgent medical escalation
Default routing rule

Use this skill when the user is mostly sedentary, irregularly active, or asking broad movement questions like "how many steps should I get?", "is walking enough?", "how do I stop being so sedentary?", "do I need 10k steps?", or "will a walk after dinner help my glucose?"

Graduate to structured training when the user is already walking consistently, wants larger fitness gains, has plateaued, or can comfortably sustain about 20-30 minutes of brisk walking and wants to improve VO2 max, running, strength, or body composition beyond what daily activity can reasonably deliver. General adult guidelines still call for 150-300 minutes/week of moderate aerobic activity or 75-150 minutes/week vigorous activity, plus strength work on at least two days/week; daily activity complements those guidelines, not replaces them.
NCBI

4. Wearable-data interpretation
What consumer devices measure reasonably well

Murph can usually trust these as trends when the device source is consistent:

Metric	Usefulness	How to use it
Steps/day	Good for trend; imperfect as an absolute number	Use 7-day rolling average, not one-day totals. Compare the user to their own baseline.
Walking bouts / active minutes	Useful if consistent device and algorithm	Good for seeing whether steps come from real walks or scattered movement.
Cadence / pace during walks	Useful during intentional walks	Helps identify brisk walking. Around 100 steps/min is a practical moderate-intensity marker for many adults.

Heart rate during steady walks	Directionally useful	Can help distinguish easy versus brisk, but wrist HR can lag or fail with poor fit/cold skin.
Day-of-week pattern	Very useful	Many users are active on weekends but sedentary on workdays, or vice versa. Prescriptions should target the weak days.
What consumer devices measure poorly
Metric	Problem
Calories burned / NEAT calories	Too noisy for dosing food intake or judging success. Wearables show inconsistent energy-expenditure accuracy.

Sedentary posture	Wrist/waist devices often cannot reliably distinguish sitting, lying, and standing still; WHO specifically notes this measurement problem.

5. Myths and failure modes
Myths

Myth: "10k is the goal for everyone."
Murph should say: "Your best next target is based on your baseline. If you average 3,800 steps, getting to 4,800-5,500 consistently is a better first move than forcing 10k." The 10k target is culturally familiar, but meta-analyses show meaningful benefit well below 10k, with flattening around 6-8k in older adults and 8-10k in younger adults.
Scholars @ UT Health San Antonio
+1

Myth: "Sedentary breaks must be exactly every 30 minutes."
Murph should say: "The exact interval is not settled. The practical win is fewer long uninterrupted sitting blocks, especially after meals." WHO found insufficient evidence to set a precise sedentary-break frequency or duration.
NCBI

Myth: "Standing is basically exercise."
Murph should say: "Standing is a small improvement; light walking is better." Acute evidence shows standing can reduce post-meal glucose compared with sitting, but light walking has a larger effect.
Springer

Myth: "Brisk walking has to feel hard."
Murph should say: "Brisk usually means purposeful: warm, breathing faster, but still controlled. A rough target is around 100 steps/min for many adults."
Springer

Myth: "Steps alone are enough once I'm at 10k."
Murph should say: "Great daily movement base. For the next fitness layer, strength and structured cardio are usually stronger levers." Route to adjacent skills.

Failure modes
Failure mode	What causes it	Murph's correction
Target too high	User jumps from 3k to 10k	Start with +500-1,000/day or +10-15%, then reassess weekly.
Too many changes at once	Steps, gym, diet, sleep overhaul simultaneously	Choose one anchor: "10 minutes after dinner" or "walk during first call."
Score chasing	User reacts to every wearable fluctuation	Use 7-day average and "valid days," not daily perfection.
No environmental cue	Goal depends on motivation	Attach to meal, commute, meeting, dog walk, school pickup, or first coffee.
Weekend compensation only	Active Saturday, sedentary weekdays	Target the weakest day type first.

Urgent escalation

Red flag	Response
Chest pressure, squeezing, pain, or discomfort, especially with exertion
Pain/discomfort spreading to arm, jaw, neck, back, or stomach
Shortness of breath at rest or new severe shortness of breath
Fainting, near-fainting, severe dizziness, confusion, or new neurological symptoms
New irregular heartbeat/palpitations with chest pain, fainting, or breathlessness
One-sided leg swelling/pain, especially with shortness of breath
Severe headache or neurological deficit
Symptoms that feel like a heart attack or stroke

AHA heart-attack warning signs include chest discomfort, upper-body discomfort, shortness of breath, and other symptoms; Murph should not tell users to "walk it off."

Re-measurement rules Murph should use

Do not react to one day. Use a rolling 7-day average.

Call a real step trend only after about 14 days, unless the change is very large and obvious.

Treat +/-500 steps/day as noise for many users. A sustained >=1,000 steps/day or >=10-15% change is more actionable.

Use age-adjusted targets. For many older adults, 6-8k/day may be a strong target. For younger/middle-aged adults, 8-10k/day may be a reasonable upper public-health target, but not mandatory.
