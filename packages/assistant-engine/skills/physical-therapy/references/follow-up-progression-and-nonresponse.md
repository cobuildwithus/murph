# Follow-up, progression, and nonresponse

Use this reference after a plan has begun. Follow-up is not a courtesy check; it is the re-examination step that determines whether the plan and working hypothesis remain appropriate.

## Follow-up data set

Collect only the information needed to make the next decision.

1. **Exposure completed**
   - exercise/activity names
   - actual sets, repetitions, time, distance, load, range, and frequency
   - deviations from the plan
2. **During-response**
   - symptom quality, location, intensity, and trend
   - movement quality and function
3. **Later-day response**
   - approximately two hours later when useful
   - swelling, stiffness, fatigue, or delayed symptoms
4. **Next-morning response**
   - pain/symptom level
   - walking, stairs, reaching, lifting, or target-task function
   - sleep effect
5. **Primary outcome**
   - repeat the same target-task baseline under similar conditions
6. **Safety re-screen**
   - new swelling, locking, giving way, neurological symptoms, fever/systemic illness, limb changes, or chest/breathing symptoms
7. **Adherence and friction**
   - what was feasible, confusing, painful, time-consuming, or irrelevant
8. **Confidence and concerns**
   - whether the user feels safer, more capable, or more worried

Do not ask all items as separate questions when the user has already described them.

## Decision hierarchy

Safety overrides symptom-improvement data. A user can report less pain and still require assessment because of new weakness, swelling, vascular change, or another red flag.

Choose one primary decision at each review.

### Progress

Use when:

- the relevant exercise or target task has been green for at least two comparable exposures
- function is improving or stable
- recovery is complete enough before the next exposure
- no new safety feature appears
- the current item still has a useful next step

Progress only one major variable. Keep the rest stable long enough to interpret the response.

### Continue unchanged

Use when:

- the plan is tolerable
- function has not worsened
- the expected trial is not complete
- the exposure frequency has been too low to judge
- the user is still learning the movement

Do not call an unchanged plan “failure” after one uneventful session.

### Regress

Use when:

- the response is yellow
- next-morning function is clearly worse
- technique deteriorates with the current dose
- recovery takes longer than intended
- the plan is too demanding for the user's current capacity

Reduce one variable by the smallest amount likely to restore a green response. A 20–50% reduction can be a practical starting heuristic after a clear overshoot, but avoid false precision.

### Substitute

Use when:

- the exercise repeatedly triggers an excessive response despite regression
- setup, equipment, privacy, or complexity makes it impractical
- the user cannot understand or perform it safely
- another movement can serve the same role with lower burden

Preserve the intervention role whenever possible.

### Revise the working hypothesis

Use when:

- symptoms appear in a new location or distribution
- the predicted load-response does not occur
- a low-risk test contradicts the working pattern
- the problem is less mechanical or less stable than expected
- the same plan repeatedly fails despite appropriate dose changes

Do not simply add more exercises. Reopen location, mechanism, associated features, and health context.

### Refer

Refer when:

- a new emergency or prompt-care feature appears
- symptoms or function are worsening despite regression
- uncertainty remains high and examination could change care
- recurrent swelling, locking, instability, or neurological symptoms persist
- the user cannot progress a meaningful task after a reasonable, well-executed trial
- sleep/work/basic daily activity remains substantially limited
- a clinician-controlled protocol, imaging decision, or formal clearance is needed

### Complete

Conclude the episode when:

- the user's target outcome is met or acceptably restored
- the user can self-progress or maintain safely
- the user understands flare rules and referral triggers
- remaining symptoms do not meaningfully limit the agreed goal, or the user chooses to stop after informed discussion

Provide a brief maintenance plan and explain when a new assessment is warranted.

## Green/yellow/red interpretation

### Green

- mild or familiar response
- no meaningful change in movement quality
- no new swelling, instability, neurological, vascular, or systemic finding
- settles toward baseline in the expected window
- next-morning function is unchanged or better

Decision: continue or progress the smallest useful variable.

### Yellow

- more symptoms than intended
- later-day or next-morning response is worse
- target task is harder, but no urgent feature exists
- movement becomes more guarded or compensatory
- recovery takes longer than planned

Decision: hold, reduce, or substitute; reassess before progressing.

### Red

- severe, rapidly increasing, electric, spreading, or unfamiliar pain
- new swelling, altered gait, instability, loss of function, numbness, weakness, fever/systemic illness, limb discoloration/coldness, or chest/breathing symptoms
- meaningful worsening persists for several days despite regression

Decision: stop the aggravating exposure, re-screen, and route care.

## Time windows

Avoid rigid universal timelines.

Use:

- **same session** for technique and immediate safety
- **later that day** for delayed symptom or swelling response
- **next morning** for recovery and function
- **after two to three comparable exposures** for early dose decisions
- **one to three weeks** as a common review window for a simple stable complaint, adjusted for irritability, exposure frequency, and goal

A user who has only performed one session has not completed a three-week trial. A user worsening rapidly should not wait for a planned review date.

## Progression dimensions

Choose one:

- range
- external load
- repetitions/time/distance
- frequency
- speed
- support
- balance demand
- impact
- fatigue
- task complexity
- environmental specificity

A 5–15% increase may be a reasonable volume heuristic for some tasks, but use the smallest practical step and the user's response rather than an automatic rule.

## Nonresponse analysis

Before declaring a plan ineffective, check:

1. **Was the working pattern plausible?**
2. **Was the care level appropriate?**
3. **Was the dose actually performed?**
4. **Was the dose too low, too high, or inconsistent?**
5. **Was the selected exercise relevant to the target task?**
6. **Was the plan understandable and feasible?**
7. **Did another load outside the plan dominate symptoms?**
8. **Did the symptom pattern change?**
9. **Are sleep, stress, fear, work, or health factors limiting progress?**
10. **Would an in-person examination change management?**

Do not frame nonadherence as the only explanation. Do not frame adherence as proof that the diagnosis was correct.

## Recurrence

For repeated episodes:

- compare the current pattern with prior episodes rather than assuming they are identical
- ask what is materially different and run only the targeted safety check triggered by that difference
- identify the earliest reproducible threshold
- review recent load and contextual changes
- simplify a flare plan that preserves tolerated activity
- refer when episodes recur at progressively lower loads, include swelling/instability/neurological signs, or fail to regain prior capacity

## Adherence redesign

When the routine is not being completed, ask a single direct question:

> What is the main thing making this hard to do: time, symptoms, uncertainty, setup, boredom, or something else?

Then adapt:

- **Time:** create a 5–10 minute minimum version.
- **Symptoms:** reduce range/load/volume or substitute.
- **Uncertainty:** clarify purpose and stop rules; demonstrate via approved media.
- **Setup/equipment:** choose an equivalent no-equipment item.
- **Boredom/relevance:** connect to the target task or choose a task-based exposure.
- **Forgetting:** use a consensual reminder tied to an existing routine.
- **Fear:** use a safer starting exposure, explain response rules, and consider clinician support.

## Follow-up response template

```text
What the response tells us:
- [green/yellow/red and why]

Primary decision:
- [progress / continue / regress / substitute / revise / refer / complete]

Change for the next exposure:
- [one variable only]

Keep stable:
- [variables not changing]

Recheck:
- [same baseline and timing]

Stop or seek care if:
- [specific off-ramp]
```
