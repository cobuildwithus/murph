# Clinical reasoning and intake

Use this reference to turn a symptom report plus relevant vault context into a safe, testable rehabilitation plan. The aim is not to imitate a physical examination with words. The aim is to reuse what is already known and collect only the smallest missing set of information that changes care level, working hypotheses, starting dose, or follow-up.

## Reasoning model

Treat each episode as a loop:

1. retrieve and reconcile relevant conversation/vault context
2. orient to the user's goal
3. resolve care level quietly
4. update the presentation
5. estimate stability and irritability
6. choose or refresh repeatable baselines
7. form ranked working hypotheses
8. use only plan-changing observations
9. select a bounded intervention
10. compare the response with the hypothesis
11. progress, revise, refer, or conclude

The user can enter at any point. Do not force them through a rigid form when enough information is already present. Read `vault-context-and-progressive-disclosure.md` before selecting questions.

## Minimum viable intake

Before a first plan, usually resolve the items below from the current conversation, vault, or one targeted question. These are information requirements, not questions that must all be asked aloud:

- exact location and distribution
- sudden versus gradual onset and meaningful mechanism
- trend: improving, stable, fluctuating, or worsening
- the most important aggravating task and dose threshold
- what happens during, later that day, and the next morning
- meaningful associated symptoms and targeted safety findings
- current function and desired activity
- relevant recent changes in load or context
- health history or clinician restrictions that alter risk
- practical constraints: time, equipment, environment, confidence

If any of these is unknown but cannot change the current decision, do not delay a bounded first step merely to complete the list.

## Question sequencing

### First perform the context pass

Use the active episode, related history, clinician instructions, prior plan/response, goal, equipment, and preferences. Mark only facts that are current enough for today's decision. Do not infer a current negative from an absent or old record.

### When enough information is already present

Ask nothing. Give the working interpretation, baseline, bounded plan, response rules, and review point. Do not repeat a disclaimer or ask for confirmation merely to make the intake look complete.

### When the complaint is vague but the vault is rich

Ask for the delta or one discriminating detail:

> I’m using the prior episode and last tolerated plan. Is this the same gradual pattern, or did it begin after a new injury?

Do not ask again about age, equipment, schedule, prior injuries, or goals when they are already relevant and current.

### When both the complaint and stored context are sparse

Ask one compact, high-value question, usually location plus onset/mechanism:

> Where exactly is the strongest symptom, and did it begin with a new injury or gradually?

Offer a safe interim action when possible. Do not append a generic list of unrelated warning signs.

### When the active pattern suggests a specific safety concern

Ask the smallest pattern-specific cluster. Examples:

- acute trauma: current ability to use/bear weight plus major deformity or rapid swelling
- radiating neurological symptoms: whether weakness/numbness is new or progressive and the one relevant emergency feature
- calf/limb symptoms with clot context: one-sided swelling/warmth plus chest/breathing symptoms
- hot swollen joint: fever/systemic illness plus recent procedure/wound

Route immediately when a positive finding already establishes the need for urgent care.

### When the user asks for exercises immediately

If the available context supports a stable low-risk pathway, give the safest reversible first action and ask no more than one personalization question. If a specific unresolved safety issue remains, ask that one question and give only a safe interim action—not a full routine.

### When the user gives a diagnosis label

Acknowledge it, then distinguish the source:

- **User label:** reopen only the missing location, mechanism, and alternatives that can change the plan.
- **Clinician diagnosis:** attribute it to the clinician; reuse the dated restrictions and current function.
- **Imaging wording:** do not assume an imaging finding is the pain source or the complete treatment target.

Useful phrasing:

> I’ll use that as one working possibility. I already have the prior goal and plan; the one thing I need is whether the current trigger or symptom pattern has changed.

## History domains

### 1. Location and distribution

Ask the user to identify the strongest point with one finger when possible, then describe the broader area.

Record:

- side: left, right, bilateral, midline, or unclear
- focal versus diffuse
- superficial versus deep
- joint line, muscle belly, tendon region, bone, or unclear—without claiming tissue localization
- whether symptoms radiate, jump, or follow a line
- whether another region such as the spine may be related

A traveling, burning, electric, numb, or tingling symptom changes the hypothesis and safety screen.

### 2. Symptom quality

Quality can guide questions but rarely proves tissue pathology.

- ache, soreness, stiffness, pressure, or familiar local discomfort may fit a stable load-related pattern
- sharp pain may occur in many benign and serious presentations; use context
- burning, electric, numbness, pins-and-needles, or weakness raises neural/referred considerations
- cramping can reflect fatigue, muscle, vascular, electrolyte, or other causes; do not overinterpret
- catching, locking, instability, collapse, or swelling requires precise clarification
- “tightness” may describe protection, fatigue, sensitivity, limited motion, or belief; do not assume shortening

### 3. Onset and mechanism

Clarify:

- date or approximate time frame
- sudden versus gradual
- trauma, twist, direct blow, forced range, heavy effort, repetitive exposure, prolonged position, illness, or no clear event
- immediate pain, pop, swelling, or loss of function
- whether the user could continue and use the area afterward
- first episode versus recurrence

The sequence often matters more than the label.

### 4. Time course

Record:

- improving, stable, fluctuating, or worsening
- constant or intermittent
- morning, daytime, evening, rest, and night behavior
- warm-up effect
- time to settle after exposure
- whether each exposure starts symptoms earlier or at a lower load

Night pain alone is nonspecific. Progressive rest/night pain combined with systemic or cancer/bone risk lowers the threshold for assessment.

### 5. Dose-response

This is often the highest-value part of a remote musculoskeletal history.

Resolve:

- exact task or position
- amount before symptoms begin
- intensity and quality during the task
- what happens if the user continues
- effect about two hours later when relevant
- next-morning function and symptoms
- recovery time to usual baseline
- whether the response is predictable

Prefer a reproducible threshold over vague statements such as “activity makes it worse.”

### 6. Function and participation

Ask what the symptom changes in:

- walking, stairs, sitting, standing, sleep, dressing, carrying, reaching, typing, lifting, balance, work, caregiving, exercise, sport, or social activity
- speed, distance, load, confidence, or technique
- avoidance and compensations

Do not assume returning to sport is the user's primary goal. A daily task may matter more.

### 7. Load and environmental context

Look for changes in:

- frequency, volume, intensity, speed, range, impact, hills, terrain, or fatigue
- work setup, repetitive demands, travel, prolonged sitting/standing, or new caregiving tasks
- footwear, equipment, bike/workstation fit, or lifting setup
- sleep, recovery, illness, nutrition, stress, or schedule

A temporal association is not proof of cause. Use it to define a reversible experiment.

### 8. Relevant health context

Retrieve proportionately from the vault first; ask only when missing and plan-changing:

- age group and developmental stage
- pregnancy/postpartum state
- recent surgery, procedure, immobilization, or long travel
- medications affecting bleeding, bone, tendon, alertness, or healing
- osteoporosis, prior stress injury, low energy availability, or prolonged steroid exposure
- cancer, infection, inflammatory disease, diabetes/neuropathy, cardiovascular disease, prior clot
- current clinician restrictions or rehabilitation protocol

Avoid collecting unrelated medical history that will not change management.

### 9. Prior care and self-management

Record:

- prior clinician diagnoses and source/date
- imaging or surgery
- exercises, rest, taping, braces, medication, manual care, or other strategies tried
- exact dose and response rather than “it did not work”
- what the user found acceptable or burdensome

Do not repeat a prior failed routine merely because it is common for the label.

## Stability and irritability

### Stability

- **Stable:** predictable, reproducible, not materially changing, no new safety feature.
- **Evolving:** trend or distribution is changing; closer review or clinician assessment may be needed.
- **Unstable:** rapidly worsening, unpredictable severe loss of function, or new neurological/vascular/systemic/traumatic concern. Do not proceed with routine self-management.

### Irritability

Estimate from three dimensions:

1. **Provocation threshold:** how little input triggers symptoms.
2. **Impact:** how much pain or function loss occurs.
3. **Recovery:** how long it takes to settle.

A high pain number with quick recovery and preserved function may be less irritable than a moderate symptom caused by minimal movement that lasts two days. Irritability guides test dose, exercise range, and review timing.

## Goal and baseline design

### Goal

Use a specific, meaningful activity and user-defined success condition.

Good goal:

> Walk 30 minutes at a normal pace without needing to shorten stride and be no worse the next morning.

Weak goal:

> Fix the joint.

### Baseline set

Use one to three measures:

- **Symptom:** current, task, and next-morning rating or symptom description.
- **Function:** user-specific activity ability/difficulty rating from 0–10.
- **Threshold:** minutes, distance, repetitions, load, range, work duration, or number of stairs.
- **Confidence:** 0–10 confidence in performing the task safely.

Store test conditions: surface, speed, load, support, time of day, and warm-up when they matter.

### Outcome-measure caution

Do not call an improvised rating a validated patient-reported outcome measure. A formal scale should be implemented with its official instructions, scoring, licensing status, and interpretation. For routine Murph follow-up, transparent user-specific measures are often sufficient.

## Working-hypothesis construction

### Use pattern classes first

Before a precise condition label, consider:

- local load-related musculoskeletal pattern
- acute traumatic/structural injury pattern
- referred or neurological pattern
- vascular pattern
- inflammatory/infectious/systemic pattern
- bone stress or fracture pattern
- persistent pain with psychosocial and contextual contributors
- unclear/mixed pattern

### Hypothesis record

For each plausible pattern, record:

- **supports**
- **against or missing**
- **cannot-miss alternatives**
- **confidence**
- **next data needed**
- **self-management suitability**

Example:

```text
Working pattern: stable local load-related shoulder presentation
Confidence: moderate
Supports: gradual onset, reproducible with overhead load, no trauma, no neurological/systemic features, daily function mostly preserved
Against/missing: exact range and next-day response not yet known
Cannot miss: referred neck/chest symptoms if distribution changes
Plan-changing observation: response to a low-load supported movement and next-morning function
Self-management: reasonable bounded trial
```

### Avoid diagnostic theater

Do not list every possible pathology. Include alternatives only when they alter safety, testing, treatment, or referral.

Do not use pseudo-precision such as “82% likely.” Confidence labels are qualitative unless a validated prediction model is actually implemented for the correct population.

## Psychosocial and contextual reasoning

Pain and disability are influenced by beliefs, fear, sleep, stress, work, support, access, and prior experience. These are not evidence that pain is imaginary.

Ask when relevant:

- “What are you most worried this might mean?”
- “Are you avoiding anything because it feels unsafe, even when it does not always hurt?”
- “How is this affecting sleep, work, mood, or confidence?”
- “What would make a plan realistic this week?”

Respond by validating the impact, correcting unsupported threat beliefs gently, and using graded exposure and functional wins. Do not interpret distress as the sole cause of symptoms or use “just stress” language.

## Selecting remote observations

Only test a question the history cannot answer.

### Useful low-risk observation families

- comfortable active range in a supported position
- walking or short task exposure
- sit-to-stand from a stable chair
- supported balance near a counter
- shallow squat or step task with support
- gentle submaximal isometric effort when appropriate
- task-specific setup review

### Required instruction structure

1. purpose of the observation
2. safe environment and support
3. exact setup
4. one attempt or small dose
5. what to notice
6. stop criteria
7. how the result changes the plan

### Never infer more than observed

A video may show trunk lean, speed, depth, apparent symmetry, or use of support. It does not prove muscle weakness, joint instability, tissue damage, pain source, or diagnostic pathology.

## Deciding whether enough information exists

Proceed with a bounded plan when:

- care level is resolved
- target task and baseline are clear
- the working pattern is sufficiently stable
- the proposed intervention is reversible and low risk
- the user can understand and perform it safely
- response and referral rules are explicit

Ask another question only when the answer could change one of those conditions and is not already available in sufficiently current context. In a normal turn, choose the single highest-value gap.

Refer when uncertainty cannot be reduced safely through conversation or low-risk observation.

## Summary template

Use an internal summary like:

```text
Goal:
- [target task and success condition]

Presentation:
- [location/distribution]
- [onset/mechanism/trend]
- [aggravating dose and recovery]
- [associated features]
- [relevant health/context]

Care level:
- [level and reason]

Baselines:
- [symptom]
- [function]
- [threshold/confidence]

Working patterns:
1. [pattern, confidence, supports, conflicts]
2. [important alternative]

Plan-changing uncertainty:
- [one item]

Next decision:
- [self-management plan / observation / referral]
```

Do not expose the full internal summary unless it helps the user or they ask for a handoff note.
