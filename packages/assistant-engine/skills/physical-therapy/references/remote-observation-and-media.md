# Remote observation, home tests, photos, and video

Use this reference before asking the user to perform a movement test or interpreting an image/video.

## Core limit

Remote observation can help describe movement, reproduce a task, compare a baseline, and choose a safer starting dose. It cannot reproduce palpation, reflexes, clinician-applied strength testing, joint mobility, vascular examination, ligament testing, imaging, or a full neurological examination.

Do not let the apparent precision of video create diagnostic certainty.

## When an observation is justified

Use a remote observation only when the result can change one of these:

- urgency or need for referral
- working-hypothesis ranking
- exercise choice
- starting range/load/support
- target-task baseline
- progression or regression

Do not test merely to appear clinical.

## Safety prerequisites

Before any movement observation, confirm or infer:

- no unresolved emergency or prompt-care feature
- stable floor and adequate space
- appropriate footwear or bare feet when safe
- a counter, wall, rail, or stable chair if balance could be challenged
- no unstable stools, rolling chairs, clutter, pets, cords, wet surfaces, or low lighting
- user can stop immediately and understands the stop criteria
- no untrained helper needs to apply force

If the environment is unsafe, choose a supported or nonmovement alternative.

## Allowed observation families

Examples that may be appropriate after screening:

- comfortable active movement within the user's chosen range
- walking a short known distance
- sit-to-stand from a stable chair
- supported single-leg stance or weight shift
- shallow supported squat or low step task
- low-dose task simulation
- gentle submaximal isometric effort without external resistance from another person
- workstation, lifting setup, or equipment-position review

Choose the least demanding test that answers the question.

## Observations to avoid remotely

Do not direct:

- forceful ligament, meniscal, or instability tests
- aggressive nerve-tension tests
- cervical artery or vertebrobasilar “self-tests”
- maximal strength or one-repetition maximum tests during pain evaluation
- repeated hopping, running, or impact when bone stress or fracture is possible
- deep loaded range after acute trauma
- tests requiring another person to push, pull, twist, or stabilize
- maneuvers with meaningful fall risk
- repeated testing of a painful joint to “confirm” a diagnosis

## Instruction format

Every home observation should include:

1. **Question:** what the observation is intended to clarify.
2. **Environment:** where and what support to use.
3. **Setup:** exact starting position.
4. **Dose:** usually one to three repetitions or a brief exposure.
5. **Notice:** location, quality, movement control, and function.
6. **Stop:** sharp/rising pain, instability, numbness/weakness, dizziness, chest/breathing symptoms, marked swelling, or feeling unsafe.
7. **Interpretation:** what each broad result changes—and what it cannot prove.

## Photo and video consent

Before requesting media:

- explain what it may help assess
- explain that it will not diagnose the condition
- ask for consent
- request the minimum view needed
- avoid capturing faces, documents, addresses, other people, or unrelated home details when possible
- do not retain incidental sensitive content
- follow product privacy and storage rules

The user should have a non-video alternative.

## Camera guidance

When video is genuinely useful:

- use a stable camera at a safe distance
- show the full relevant body segment and support surface
- avoid hazardous repositioning during recording
- perform only the agreed low-dose movement
- stop after one clear attempt if the result is visible

Do not ask the user to balance while holding the phone.

## Interpretation language

Safe:

- “I can see that you shorten the step as symptoms appear.”
- “The right side appears to use more hand support in this clip.”
- “This angle does not show the foot clearly enough to compare.”
- “That observation suggests we should start with more support.”

Unsafe:

- “Your glute is weak.”
- “Your meniscus is torn.”
- “Your pelvis is rotated.”
- “This proves the nerve is trapped.”
- “Your joint is unstable.”

Describe visible behavior, not hidden pathology.

## Measurement limits

Do not provide exact angles, asymmetry percentages, or force estimates unless the product uses a validated measurement method with known error and appropriate camera calibration.

When comparing videos over time, keep conditions similar:

- camera placement
- footwear
- support
- load
- speed
- warm-up
- task depth/distance

Even then, treat change as one outcome signal rather than proof of tissue healing.

## Media-derived data storage

Store only plan-relevant observations, such as:

- “uses hand support for sit-to-stand”
- “symptoms begin on the fourth step”
- “movement stopped because of instability sensation”

Do not store unrelated appearance, home, body, identity, or psychological details. Do not infer diagnoses from media in the health record.

## Failure behavior

If media is unclear, incomplete, or unsafe:

- say what cannot be determined
- do not guess
- choose a safer text-based baseline or recommend in-person assessment

If the video reveals a red-flag pattern, stop movement guidance and route care.
