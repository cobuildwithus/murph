# Deciding who needs to be seen

Read this when a new or changed pain complaint carries a worrying cue, or before recommending in-person care. The emergency patterns are listed in the main skill file. This file covers the two levels below an emergency, the one question worth asking for each pattern, and what to do when the member says no to care.

Use what the member has already said and current records first. Ask only the single most useful missing question for the pattern in front of you. A finding raises concern and does not make a diagnosis, so weigh the combination, how severe it is, how fast it is changing and the member's health background. One vague item from the past is not a reason to refer. Several together, or a high-risk background, are.

## Seen in person today or soon

Recommend prompt in-person assessment, and hold the exercise plan until then, for any of these:

- Unexplained swelling, warmth, redness or tenderness in one limb, especially after surgery, a cast, long travel, pregnancy, cancer, estrogen treatment or an earlier clot.
- Being unable to bear weight or use the limb after an injury.
- Large or fast swelling or bruising.
- A joint that truly locks, repeatedly gives way, or has lost a lot of movement after an injury.
- New foot drop, weakness that is getting worse, numbness that is spreading, or new clumsiness or loss of balance.
- A possible fracture or bone stress injury: pain at one spot on a bone, pain with impact that starts earlier each time, pain at rest or at night, especially with osteoporosis, long steroid use, eating too little, missed periods or a recent jump in training.
- A sudden snap or pop with a visible change in shape or an immediate loss of a tendon's function.
- Hot, red swelling, a fever, an open wound, a recent procedure or a weakened immune system.
- New severe pain while taking a blood thinner or with a bleeding disorder.
- Constant pain that does not change with movement, together with a history of cancer, unexplained weight loss or night sweats.
- Anything Murph cannot assess safely, or a plan the member cannot carry out safely.

## A routine visit

Recommend a clinician or physical therapist, and keep helping in the meantime, when an examination is likely to change the plan:

- The source of the pain is still unclear after a focused history.
- Substantial loss of function, repeated episodes, or disturbed sleep or work continue.
- Neurological symptoms are stable and unresolved.
- Swelling, locking, catching or giving way keeps coming back.
- A plan that was actually carried out has not helped, or symptoms are getting worse.
- Another health condition materially changes exercise risk.
- The member wants a diagnosis, an imaging decision, paperwork or formal clearance.

## The one question for each pattern

| Pattern in the message | Worth asking if unknown |
|---|---|
| A fall, twist, blow or pop | Can they use the limb or bear weight now, and is there a deformity or fast swelling? |
| Pain that travels, numbness or tingling | Is there new or worsening weakness, a change in walking or hand control, or any change in bladder, bowel or sensation around the saddle area? |
| Calf or limb pain after surgery, a cast or long travel | Is one side swollen or warm, and is there any chest pain or shortness of breath? |
| A hot or quickly swollen joint | Is there a fever or feeling unwell, or a recent procedure or wound? |
| Pain at one spot on a bone with impact | Is it now present at rest or at night, and is there a bone-health risk? |
| After surgery or a procedure | What does the current protocol restrict, and is there any new sign of a complication? |

## Three things never to do

- Do not suggest massaging or exercising a limb when a blood clot is possible.
- Do not let the member drive themselves when they may faint, have severe neurological loss or cannot move safely. Tell them to call emergency services.
- Do not give exercises, stretches or home tests while urgent care is pending.

## How to say it

Name the specific finding that drives the recommendation, say how soon to be seen, and say which symptoms would make it an emergency. For a self-managed trial, describe the plan and its stopping points and add no general caveat about what cannot be ruled out.

## When the member declines care

Repeat the recommendation and the specific concern once, without blame. Do not supply the exercise, massage or test they asked for when it could delay urgent care. Give the short list of symptoms that would make it an emergency, and help with practical barriers such as transport or finding a local urgent-care service.

If the member expresses hopelessness, thoughts of self-harm or being unable to stay safe, stop the rehabilitation conversation and read `stress-regulation` for the crisis guidance. Do not treat it as a motivation problem.

## Check again when things change

Look at the level of care again when symptoms change materially, a new body area or neurological symptom appears, swelling, fever, giving way or night pain develops, there is a new injury, procedure or medicine, or progress stalls in a way the working explanation does not predict. An earlier low-risk reading does not clear later symptoms.
