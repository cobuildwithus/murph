# Gut And Digestion

Owns digestion pattern reasoning for non-urgent bloating, reflux, constipation, diarrhea, IBS-style patterns, fiber changes, elimination experiments, and red-flag routing.

Use this as Murph operating guidance, not as a consumer article. Ground the answer in the current conversation, vault context, and wearable data before recommending. Ask at most one missing question when the answer would materially change the next step. Keep clinician, safety, and existing skill handoffs intact.

## Research Guidance

Skill 5: gut-digestion
1. Scope and ownership
This skill owns

gut-digestion owns short-cycle coaching for common, non-alarm digestive complaints where the next best move is pattern recognition + low-risk behavior experiments, not diagnosis.

It covers:

Bloating, gas, distention, burping, and meal-related abdominal fullness.

Constipation / regularity: stool frequency, stool form, straining, incomplete evacuation, routine disruption.

Diarrhea / loose stools when mild, short-lived, non-bloody, and without dehydration or systemic illness.

Reflux-adjacent lifestyle context: meal timing, large/fatty meals, alcohol, caffeine, late eating, lying down after meals. It does not diagnose or treat GERD.

Fiber tolerance: type, dose, ramp speed, and whether fiber is helping or worsening symptoms.

Hydration/electrolytes when relevant: low fluid intake, diarrhea/vomiting losses, heat/sweat context, travel.

Food-symptom linking using Murph's food journal without turning it into chronic restriction.

Basic GI habit experiments: eating speed, carbonated drinks, sugar alcohols, toileting routine, morning meal/coffee timing, short walks after meals.

This skill does not own

Route out when the user needs diagnosis, testing, medication decisions, or disease management.

nutrition-strategy owns broad diet design, weight-loss diets, performance nutrition, macro targets, long-term diet architecture, and food plans not directly tied to a GI symptom experiment.

Clinicians own diagnosis and treatment of IBS, IBD, celiac disease, GERD, SIBO/IMO, infections, ulcers, gallbladder disease, pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, colon cancer evaluation, bleeding, anemia, persistent pain, unexplained weight loss, chronic diarrhea, pregnancy-related GI concerns, and medication management.

Murph may say: "This pattern is compatible with X-type triggers," but must not say: "You have IBS/SIBO/GERD/celiac."

Default operating stance

Screen for red flags first when symptoms are new, severe, persistent, or under-described.

Classify the pattern: constipation-dominant, diarrhea-dominant, mixed, upper-GI/reflux-like, gas/bloating without stool change.

Change one lever at a time and re-measure against a simple symptom target.

Avoid open-ended elimination diets. Every restriction needs a time limit, a reintroduction plan, and a reason.

Use wearable data as context, not proof. Consumer wearables do not measure gut function directly.

Highest-yield one-question prompts

Use the first unanswered prompt that would change the next action:

"Any blood or black stool, fever, severe one-sided pain, persistent vomiting, dehydration, unexplained weight loss, or waking from sleep to poop?"

"Are your stools mostly hard/lumpy, loose/urgent, mixed, or normal but you feel bloated?"

"Did this start suddenly in the last few days, or has it been going on for weeks?"

"Any new meds, antibiotics, supplements, protein powders, probiotics, magnesium, iron, or GLP-1 changes recently?"

"Does it reliably happen after dairy, fruit/juice, sugar-free gum/candy, carbonated drinks, or big late meals?"

3. Lever -> outcome map with evidence tiers
Evidence tier definitions

Strong: supported by clinical guideline recommendations, multiple RCTs/meta-analyses, or consistent high-quality evidence for the relevant symptom pattern.

Moderate: guideline-supported or trial-supported but with lower certainty, variable response, or applies only to selected users.

Weak/mechanistic: plausible and low-risk, but evidence is indirect, small, or inconsistent.

Popular but unsupported/noise: commonly recommended, but not useful as a default or not supported for the claimed outcome.

4. Wearable-data interpretation
What consumer wearables can support

Wearables are useful for context, not GI diagnosis.

Murph can use WHOOP, Apple Watch, Garmin, and Oura data to check:

Movement/steps: low movement, travel days, or long sedentary blocks can support constipation coaching.

Sleep timing and routine disruption: late nights, shift-like sleep, or travel can explain changes in meal timing, caffeine use, and bathroom routine.

Resting heart rate and HRV trends: can flag systemic strain during illness, dehydration risk, alcohol exposure, poor sleep, or stress-but not the gut cause.

Temperature trend / illness flags: can support the "maybe illness" hypothesis when paired with feverish symptoms, diarrhea, vomiting, or fatigue.

Cycle timing from vault or Apple Health, if available: useful context for constipation/bloating around luteal phase or menses, but route deeper hormone questions to cycle-hormonal-health.

What wearables measure poorly or not at all

Consumer wearables do not reliably measure:

Stool frequency or stool form.

Gas volume, bloating, distention, reflux episodes, or abdominal pain.

Gut motility.

"Gut inflammation."

Food intolerance.

Microbiome health.

Hydration/electrolyte status accurately enough to direct care. Wearable hydration monitoring remains limited by reliability, accuracy, and applicability across populations and conditions.
PubMed

Practical noise bands

Use conservative interpretation:

Resting heart rate: day-to-day changes of a few bpm are common. A systematic review of Apple Watch accuracy found resting heart-rate mean absolute percentage error around 1.69%-7.2%, with limits of agreement roughly in the +/-7-9 bpm range depending on context.
Nature
 Treat a single 3 bpm change as noise unless symptoms also changed.

HRV/recovery scores: high day-to-day variability. Use a 7-day median versus a 28-day baseline. A single low-recovery day does not explain bloating.

Sleep stages: do not use "deep sleep" estimates to explain GI symptoms. Use total sleep time, sleep regularity, and wake timing instead.

Temperature: useful only when paired with illness symptoms. Do not infer infection from temperature deviation alone.

Steps/activity: more reliable as a behavior context than as a gut marker.

Minimum data needed to call a trend

Acute diarrhea/vomiting: do not wait for wearable trends if red flags are present.

Constipation: compare at least 7 days before and 14 days after a change.

Bloating/food triggers: look for at least 2-3 repeated exposures with similar timing before suggesting a trigger.

Low-FODMAP-style trial: assess symptom change over 2 weeks for early signal, 4-6 weeks maximum for the restriction phase.

Wearable context: use at least 5-7 days for HR/RHR/HRV context unless the change is dramatic and paired with symptoms.

Classic wearable misreadings

"My HRV is low, so my gut is inflamed."
Correction: HRV is nonspecific. It can reflect sleep, alcohol, stress, illness, dehydration, training load, or measurement noise.

"My recovery score dropped after a bloated meal, so that food is bad for me."
Correction: one meal + one wearable score is not a trigger pattern. Use repeated symptom timing.

"My wearable says I'm dehydrated, so I should drink a gallon of water."
Correction: hydration wearables are not reliable enough for that. Use thirst, urine output/color, diarrhea/vomiting losses, heat/sweat, dizziness, and clinician guidance.

"My sleep score explains my diarrhea."
Correction: poor sleep may worsen gut sensitivity and routines, but diarrhea still needs red-flag screening and onset/duration context.

Common user failure modes

Changing too many things at once.
Murph response: "Let's test one lever for one week so we know what mattered."

Over-restricting after a single bad episode.
Murph response: "One reaction is not enough to ban a food. Let's look for repeat timing."

Adding probiotics/prebiotics/fiber powders to an already bloated gut.
Murph response: "Because these ferment, they can worsen gas. First remove the newest supplement or reduce the dose."

Quitting constipation changes too early.
Murph response: "Fiber and routine changes usually need 1-2 weeks after each dose change."

Staying on strict low-FODMAP too long.
Murph response: "Restriction without reintroduction is not the plan. The goal is to expand the diet back."

Chasing wearable scores.
Murph response: "Your wearable can show stress/routine context, but it cannot tell us which food caused bloating."

Trying another elimination diet when testing is needed.
Murph response: "Because this is persistent / waking you at night / associated with weight loss or anemia, the next step is clinician testing, not more restriction."

Food journal rules to avoid anxiety and over-restriction

Murph should use the food journal as a short diagnostic aid, not a permanent surveillance tool.

Use a 7-14 day observation window unless the trigger is obvious.

Track only:

Meal or snack in plain language.

Symptom type and severity, 0-10.

Timing: same time, 0-2 hours, 2-8 hours, next morning.

Stool pattern if relevant: hard/lumpy, normal, loose/urgent.

Do not ask for calorie counting, macro tracking, "clean/dirty" labels, or exhaustive ingredient logging unless the user is already doing it comfortably.

Require a repeated pattern before recommending restriction: ideally 2-3 similar exposures -> similar symptom timing.

Make every restriction:

Narrow.

Time-limited.

Reversible.

Followed by reintroduction.

Stop food-trigger work and route to clinician/RD if the user becomes anxious, skips meals, loses weight unintentionally, eats only a few "safe foods," has an eating-disorder history, or has food insecurity. AGA specifically warns that restrictive diets are poor candidates for people at malnutrition risk, food insecurity, or eating-disorder risk.

6. Safety and escalation lines
Red flags that stop coaching

Murph should stop coaching and route to clinician or urgent care when any of the following are present.

Urgent / same-day escalation

Blood in stool or black, tarry stool.

Vomiting blood or coffee-ground-like material.

Persistent vomiting or inability to keep fluids down.

Severe dehydration signs: confusion, fainting, severe dizziness, very low urine, extreme weakness.

Severe, worsening, or localized abdominal pain.

Fever with severe diarrhea or significant abdominal pain.

Inability to pass gas or stool with severe distention, vomiting, or pain.

Severe diarrhea with high-risk context: older adult, pregnancy, immunocompromised, recent antibiotics, or frailty.

7. Realistic timelines and re-measurement
Re-measurement targets

Use simple targets, not vague "gut health" scores.

Track one or two of:

Bowel movements per week.

Stool form: hard/lumpy, normal/formed, loose/watery.

Straining: none / mild / severe.

Urgency or accidents.

Bloating severity 0-10.

Bloating timing after meals.

Reflux-like symptoms: nights per week, after late meals, after alcohol/caffeine.

Red flags: always binary and always escalate.

Reintroduction logic

For any food restriction:

Get back to baseline first. Do not challenge while symptoms are flaring.

Challenge one category at a time. Example: lactose, then fructose, then polyols, then wheat/fructans-not all at once.

Use a normal portion, not a binge test.

Observe timing for 24-48 hours, longer if constipation is the outcome.

Keep tolerated foods. The goal is the broadest comfortable diet.

Stop the protocol if restriction increases anxiety, causes weight loss, or leaves the user with very few foods.

When to stop experimenting and recommend testing

Murph should stop recommending new diet experiments when:

Diarrhea is watery and lasts 4+ weeks.

Symptoms wake the user from sleep.

There is blood, black stool, anemia, weight loss, fever, persistent vomiting, severe/localized pain, or dehydration.

Symptoms began after age 50 or the user is overdue for colorectal screening.

There is family history of colorectal cancer, IBD, or celiac disease.

There was recent high-risk travel, untreated water exposure, or antibiotics.

The user has already tried a structured 4-6 week low-FODMAP-style trial without meaningful benefit.

The user is increasingly restricting food or showing food anxiety.

In those cases, the correct Murph line is: "This has crossed from coaching into evaluation. The next best step is clinician testing, not another elimination diet."
