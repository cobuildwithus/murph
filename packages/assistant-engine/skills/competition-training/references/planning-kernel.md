# Planning kernel

Use this reference for a multiweek plan, phase decision, progression, disruption, taper, or detailed weekly structure. It is a decision framework, not a calendar generator.

## Plan from four truths

A defensible plan starts with:

1. what the event demands
2. what the user has recently tolerated
3. what currently limits the goal
4. what their real week can support

The event sets direction. Recent tolerated exposure sets the starting dose. The limiter sets priority. Real life sets the architecture.

## Feasibility states

Use plain-language states, not a proprietary score.

### Build normally

The target is broadly compatible with current exposure, health, skill, access, and time. Progress while monitoring response.

### Build with constraints

The event is possible, but one or more gaps require a conservative scope, modified goal, additional supervision, equipment/access solution, or explicit checkpoint.

### Completion or reduced-scope only

The performance goal is not supported by the runway or exposure. Focus on a safe completion strategy, shorter division/distance, relay/partner option, run-walk or conservative pacing, or another defined reduction.

### Reconsider or seek clearance

Safety, severe mismatch, absent prerequisite skill, unresolved symptoms, or clinician restrictions make ordinary progression inappropriate. Name the blocker and the evidence needed to revisit it.

Do not confuse registration with readiness or caution with failure.

## Timeline is a constraint, not a phase prescription

These bands are orientation only:

- **Days to about three weeks:** preserve health, reduce uncertainty, practice logistics and pacing, maintain familiar work, and taper as needed. Do not chase new fitness with overload.
- **About four to eight weeks:** choose one or two decisive gaps, build from current exposure, and include enough specificity to reduce surprise. Large foundational deficits cannot be compressed safely.
- **About nine to twenty weeks:** usually enough room for foundation/build/specific/taper sequencing, though the exact order depends on the baseline and event.
- **More than twenty weeks:** use staged goals, general development, and periodic specific blocks. Avoid keeping the athlete in peak-specific training for months.

A plan may skip, revisit, or overlap phases. Use phase objectives and exit evidence rather than dates alone.

## Phase decisions

| Phase | Primary job | Exit evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Recover/assess | restore normal easy function; clarify symptoms, baseline, and next target | ordinary easy work and daily function are stable enough to build |
| Foundation | make frequency, easy work, strength, and basic skill repeatable | the intended week is sustainable and key prerequisites are no longer fragile |
| Build | improve the main capacity limiter | relevant work is progressing without accumulating unacceptable fatigue or symptoms |
| Specific | transfer capacity to event sequence, terrain, standards, equipment, fueling, and decisions | the athlete can rehearse decisive demands with controlled cost and confidence |
| Taper | shed fatigue while retaining readiness and familiarity | event day |
| Review/recover | restore, interpret, and choose the next direction | recovery is adequate and the next decision is clear |

Do not stay in a phase because its calendar box is unfinished. Do not leave because of one good workout.

## Select the current limiter

A limiter should explain why the goal is not yet reliable and should change the next block. Useful categories include:

- aerobic durability
- sustainable speed or power
- repeated-effort recovery
- high-intensity speed/power
- strength or strength endurance
- grip or local muscular endurance
- technical skill or movement validity
- transitions or equipment handling
- terrain/eccentric tolerance
- pacing and decision quality
- fueling/GI tolerance
- training reliability

Choose one primary limiter and, at most, one support limiter. If everything is a priority, nothing is.

Distinguish:

- **capacity limiter:** cannot yet produce or sustain the demand
- **execution limiter:** has the capacity but loses it through pacing, skill, logistics, rules, or decisions
- **access limiter:** lacks time, terrain, equipment, supervision, or opportunity
- **recovery limiter:** cannot absorb the planned work

The cheapest valid fix often differs by type.

## Build the week from roles

Use roles rather than fixed weekday templates:

- priority capacity session
- priority duration/specificity session
- strength/durability or technical session
- easy/recovery volume
- optional support work
- complete rest or very low-load recovery

The number of priority sessions depends on training age, event, phase, and recovery. Many users need only one or two; well-trained athletes may tolerate more. A hard session is one that meaningfully consumes recovery, not merely one labeled intervals.

### Ordering rules

- Protect quality by separating demanding sessions enough for the athlete to execute them.
- Avoid stacking high-impact, high-eccentric, high-load, and high-intensity stress without a clear reason and demonstrated tolerance.
- Put high-skill or high-consequence work before severe fatigue unless competition-specific fatigue is the intended, safe, rehearsed stimulus.
- When time is scarce, preserve frequency or the decisive skill with shorter sessions before making every remaining session harder.
- Combine compatible work when it reduces burden without degrading the priority; separate it when interference or safety matters.

### Flexible-week design

When the schedule is unstable, define:

- **protect:** the one or two sessions that carry the block objective
- **move:** low-cost work that can shift within the week
- **drop:** optional work that disappears first
- **do not combine:** sessions whose collision creates excess risk or ruins quality

This is usually more useful than a perfect Monday-to-Sunday grid.

## Training work types

### Easy aerobic or low-cost technique

Purpose: aerobic support, frequency, recovery, technical repetition, and volume that does not consume the next priority session.

Prescription: conversational or clearly below the first meaningful threshold; mode-specific technique remains stable. Do not let wearable zones overrule obvious strain or context.

### Extended durability

Purpose: tolerate event-relevant duration, terrain, position, impact, fueling, or concentration.

Prescription: based on time and recent exposure, not a symbolic percentage of race distance. The longest session should answer a preparation question, not prove worth.

### Sustainable race-specific or threshold work

Purpose: improve the ability to hold a demanding but controlled output and calibrate event effort.

Prescription: intervals or continuous work that preserve intended pace/power/technique. End before the session becomes an unrelated survival test.

### High-intensity speed or power

Purpose: improve high-end aerobic power, speed, acceleration, repeated surges, or short-event demands.

Prescription: enough recovery and technical control to keep the target quality. It is not mandatory in every phase or for every beginner.

### Strength and durability

Purpose: force production, economy, tissue capacity, obstacle/station performance, posture/position tolerance, and robustness.

Prescription: prioritize a few event-relevant patterns; progress technique and load; avoid unnecessary soreness near key endurance work or competition. Strength supports endurance but does not replace event practice.

### Skill and transition

Purpose: make safe, valid, and efficient execution more automatic.

Prescription: frequent low-cost practice can be more useful than rare exhausted practice. Add fatigue only when the athlete can already execute the skill safely and the event requires it.

### Controlled simulation

Purpose: integrate pacing, sequence, standards, equipment, fueling, transitions, and decision rules.

Prescription: simulate only enough to answer a question. Full-event rehearsals are often too costly, especially for long or damaging events.

## Intensity distribution without dogma

Endurance evidence supports substantial low-intensity work and a smaller dose of demanding work, but does not establish one distribution for every athlete, sport, phase, or training volume.

Use these rules:

- keep easy work truly absorbable
- choose demanding work for the limiter, not because a named model is fashionable
- count strength, technical risk, competitions, and life stress in the recovery budget
- avoid accidental threshold-heavy weeks created by racing easy sessions
- use the user's response and phase objective to adjust the mix

Do not tell a recreational athlete that an elite percentage distribution is a universal requirement. Do not tell a serious athlete that all moderate work is junk.

## Progression

### Start from exposure history

Look back roughly four to eight weeks, with extra weight on the most recent repeatable weeks. Consider frequency, duration, intensity, terrain, impact, load, technique, and recovery - not just total volume.

### Progress a decision, not a number

Increase the element most likely to improve the limiter:

- frequency
- duration
- density
- pace/power/effort
- terrain or elevation
- external load
- technical complexity
- integration under fatigue
- competition density

Change one primary element when possible. Other variables may move slightly; the point is legibility, not pretending they are independent.

### Repeat before escalating

Repeat a useful exposure when the athlete needs execution confidence, technical consistency, fueling evidence, or confirmation of recovery. Novelty is not progression.

### No universal safety percentage

A fixed weekly percentage cannot account for training age, mode, intensity, impact, terrain, illness, sleep, or prior tolerance. Use it neither as a guarantee nor as an automatic veto.

### Session-spike check

Before a large or novel session, ask:

- How different is this from recent duration, intensity, impact, terrain, load, or skill exposure?
- Can the athlete execute the technical and fueling demands safely?
- What important training follows, and is the likely recovery cost acceptable?
- Could a smaller rehearsal answer the same question?

## Adapt from response

Use the minimum evidence that can change the plan:

- completion and intended execution, not just whether a box was checked
- RPE/effort and whether it drifted unusually
- pain or symptom change during and after
- next-day function and recovery
- sleep and life stress pattern
- confidence in the event-specific demand

### Green

The target work was repeatable, symptoms stable, and recovery acceptable. Repeat when confidence or technical consistency is still valuable; otherwise progress one lever.

### Yellow

The response was mixed: unusual strain, declining execution, rising fatigue, small symptom change, disrupted sleep/life, or inconsistent skill. Hold, reduce, or separate one lever. Preserve the block's main purpose.

### Red

There is meaningful symptom escalation, illness, abnormal recovery failure, or inability to complete normal easy work. Stop progression; use the appropriate health skill or professional evaluation.

A single poor day is not a trend. A repeated pattern should not be dismissed as low motivation.

## Disruption rules

### One missed session

Do not repay it automatically. Continue the week if the phase objective is still represented; move it only when spacing and recovery remain sound.

### Several misses

Reassess feasibility, schedule, session size, and recovery. Protect the highest-value exposure, shrink optional volume, and use `behavior-followthrough` when implementation rather than programming is the main issue.

### Illness

Do not make up training during or immediately after illness. Resume in stages based on symptom severity, normal daily function, easy exercise response, and any clinician guidance. Chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness, palpitations with concerning symptoms, or persistent deterioration require medical evaluation rather than a return plan from chat.

### Travel or life-stress spike

Preserve sleep, safe movement, and one priority only when it fits. Convert the week to maintenance instead of forcing progression in a poor context.

### Heat, altitude, unfamiliar terrain, or open water

Treat the environment as additional load and skill risk. Reduce intensity/duration initially, build exposure progressively when appropriate, verify event safety information, and do not equate indoor fitness with environmental readiness.

### New pain or loss of function

Use `physical-therapy` before prescribing around it. Do not solve a diagnostic gap by merely changing exercise selection.

## Benchmarks and tests

Use a test only when its result changes training or execution. Prefer repeatable, low-risk, event-relevant benchmarks over frequent maximal testing.

A benchmark should have:

- a decision it informs
- standardized enough conditions to compare meaningfully
- a clear stop rule
- an interpretation that considers fatigue, environment, equipment, and learning
- a next action for better, unchanged, or worse results

Do not use a benchmark to manufacture confidence when the event skill, duration, or logistics remain unpracticed.

## Completion versus performance

### Completion

Bias toward:

- repeatable frequency and durability
- safe skill competence
- conservative progression
- cutoffs and logistics
- fueling/hydration tolerance where relevant
- run-walk, gearing, scaling, or pacing strategies that preserve the finish
- confidence from successful representative work

Do not imitate an elite plan with fewer sessions; redesign around completion risk.

### Performance

Bias toward:

- explicit limiter development
- event-specific intensity and fatigue resistance
- stronger benchmarks and execution calibration
- selective simulations
- more precise recovery and fueling integration
- enough general work to support specificity

Do not turn every session into race pace.

### Qualification or podium

Add:

- verified current standard and eligibility rules
- quantified but uncertainty-aware gap analysis
- season/race selection and competition opportunities
- high-fidelity execution review
- qualified coach, medical, nutrition, or technical support where stakes and complexity justify it

Do not guarantee qualification or conceal that the current gap may be too large for the runway.

## Taper

The taper should reduce accumulated fatigue without erasing familiarity, rhythm, or confidence.

### Inputs

- event duration and damage cost
- recent load and fatigue
- training history
- frequency tolerance
- travel, environment, and logistics
- whether the athlete tends to feel stale or remains fatigued

### Direction

Typically reduce total volume and remove unnecessary fatigue while retaining some familiar intensity, frequency, technique, and race-specific cues. The magnitude and duration vary; evidence does not make one exact formula universal.

Avoid:

- last-minute fitness tests or catch-up blocks
- unfamiliar workouts, equipment, fueling, or supplements
- complete inactivity by default
- high-soreness strength or damaging terrain close to the event
- changing the plan because normal taper sensations feel strange after one day

### Taper troubleshooting

- **Still fatigued:** remove low-value volume and damaging work; protect sleep and basic rhythm.
- **Feels stale:** add a brief familiar activation, not a fitness test.
- **Anxious:** rehearse the plan and controllable cues; do not add training to reduce uncertainty.
- **Travel disrupted:** protect arrival, hydration/food access, light movement, and equipment checks before optional sessions.

## Weekly role examples

These are role sets, not fixed plans.

### Continuous endurance

- sustainable-intensity or economy session
- extended durability session
- strength/durability
- easy aerobic frequency
- optional technique/strides and recovery

### Multi-discipline

- key session in the current limiting discipline
- extended session in the event-dominant discipline
- short transition or integrated rehearsal
- strength/skill
- easy work across disciplines according to access and recovery

### Hybrid/obstacle

- locomotion quality or durability
- strength/station or obstacle skill
- controlled integrated session
- easy aerobic work
- grip/tissue durability and recovery

### Repeated heat/functional fitness

- largest domain-gap session
- high-skill practice while fresh
- broad conditioning or strength support
- selected repeated-event rehearsal
- recovery and mobility as needed, not as punishment

## Race-specific block

Specificity should rise only after enough foundation exists to absorb it. Choose a few representative exposures that answer:

- Can the athlete sustain the likely effort pattern?
- Can they execute the decisive skill/transition/standard?
- Can they use the intended equipment and fueling under realistic conditions?
- Can they recover or change modes as the event requires?
- Can they follow the pacing and contingency plan when fatigue appears?

Do not reproduce the event so often that rehearsal replaces development.

## Post-event transition

Immediately prioritize safety, food/fluid as tolerated, warmth/cooling, sleep, and practical care. Delay detailed judgment until acute emotion and fatigue settle.

Then decide:

- what transferred from training
- where preparation did not match the demand
- whether the main failure was capacity, execution, access, or recovery
- one training change and one execution change
- how much recovery is warranted by duration, damage, symptoms, and upcoming goals

No result requires punishment training.

## Final plan check

- Is the target feasible under the current scope?
- Does the plan start from recent tolerated exposure?
- Is one primary limiter driving the block?
- Are priority sessions few enough to recover from?
- Is easy work truly easy enough?
- Are skill and risk progressed safely?
- Are fueling, equipment, rules, and environment rehearsed only as needed?
- Are adaptation, missed-session, and stop rules explicit?
- Is the plan simpler than a generic calendar would be?
