# Performance psychology

Use this reference when confidence, pressure, attention, perfectionism, burnout risk, event-specific mental practice, or athlete development materially changes the answer.

Do not use it to create a personality profile, mental-readiness score, diagnosis, or second habit system.

## Ownership boundary

Competition training owns:

- the psychological demands of the event
- process goals and attention cues
- confidence built from relevant preparation
- pre-performance, reset, and contingency routines
- pressure rehearsal
- post-session and post-event learning

`behavior-followthrough` owns:

- repeated-behavior anchors
- standard/tiny/fallback versions
- reminder and support style
- repeated-miss repair
- privacy boundaries for social support
- fading support when stable

Identify at most one or two reliability-critical behaviors here. Hand off their implementation instead of duplicating COM-B, reminders, streak policy, or automation logic.

## Define elite behaviorally

Elite is not a label Murph awards. It is a trainable operating pattern:

**prepare -> execute -> observe -> adjust -> own**

- **Prepare:** know the purpose, cues, and likely obstacles.
- **Execute:** commit to the current task rather than evaluate identity.
- **Observe:** collect enough evidence to distinguish capacity, execution, and context.
- **Adjust:** change the smallest useful variable without punishment.
- **Own:** understand the decision well enough to need less external control next time.

The product goal is capable autonomy, not maximum engagement with Murph.

## Start from the event's mental demands

Ask what the athlete must do under pressure, not "how mentally tough are you?"

Decision-changing demands may include:

- hold back early despite excitement or pack pressure
- stay technically valid while breathing hard
- recover attention after an error, penalty, missed obstacle, or bad split
- tolerate uncertainty in long events or unknown-task formats
- make equipment, pacing, fueling, or stop decisions under fatigue
- execute repeated heats after an earlier disappointment
- communicate with a partner/team
- distinguish safe discomfort from a stop condition

Select tools for the demand. Generic motivation speeches are not mental training.

## Goal architecture

Use three layers when useful:

- **Outcome goal:** placing, time, qualification, or completion. Motivating but not fully controllable.
- **Performance goal:** a measurable standard such as pace range, valid-rep rate, transition time, or fueling execution.
- **Process goal:** the next controllable action or attention cue.

A strong competition plan uses the outcome to choose direction, performance goals to calibrate preparation, and process goals to control attention.

Do not imply that process compliance guarantees the outcome. Do not let the outcome consume every training day.

## Confidence should be calibrated

Confidence is most useful when it answers a specific question: "Can I execute this demand under these conditions?"

Build it from:

1. completed relevant repetitions
2. improving skill or benchmark evidence
3. solved problems and rehearsed contingencies
4. credible feedback from a qualified coach or clear data
5. accurate interpretation of normal arousal and fatigue

Use confidence statements tied to evidence:

- "You held the opening cap and finished the last two reps cleanly."
- "You have practiced this transition three times without equipment errors."
- "The long session exposed a fueling issue, and the revised version worked once; it needs another rehearsal."

Avoid empty certainty, identity flattery, guarantees, and "you can do anything" language.

When confidence is too low, reduce uncertainty with representative practice. When confidence is unjustifiably high, add a safe test or contingency - not humiliation.

## Deliberate practice

For a skill or execution limiter:

- define the exact error or decision
- practice a task just beyond current reliable ability
- give immediate, specific feedback where possible
- repeat while quality remains high enough to learn
- add speed, load, fatigue, or pressure only after basic execution is stable
- review one correction, not ten

Do not call random suffering deliberate practice. Difficulty without feedback or a target error is only difficulty.

## Pre-performance routine

A routine should be short enough to survive a real competition and practiced often enough to feel familiar.

A compact pattern:

1. **Check:** one equipment/body/environment check.
2. **Settle:** one breath or arousal action.
3. **Aim:** one process cue for the next segment.
4. **Commit:** begin without further analysis.

Examples:

- start line: exhale, shoulders loose, first-kilometer cap
- lift: feet, brace, one technical cue, commit
- obstacle/station: identify standard, smooth first rep, settle rhythm
- transition: follow the same equipment order, then leave

Do not add many affirmations or steps. The routine is an attention tool, not superstition.

## Reset routine

Use after a mistake, unexpected split, penalty, equipment problem, failed rep, or surge:

1. name the fact without judgment
2. take one regulating breath or physical reset
3. choose the next controllable cue
4. resume the current plan or trigger the B/C plan

Example: "Penalty happened. Long exhale. Smooth run to the next station. Recalculate after it."

The goal is faster return to useful attention, not pretending the event is going well.

## Pressure training

Pressure rehearsal should be specific, bounded, and safe. Add only enough consequence or observation to reproduce the relevant attentional problem.

Options:

- timed but submaximal execution
- a coach/judge enforcing movement standards
- a planned head-to-head start or pack position
- one attempt after a realistic wait
- a short transition after event-like effort
- a mock heat followed by a defined recovery window
- equipment checks under a clock

Debrief what attention did, not whether the athlete "has nerves."

Do not manufacture public shame, punitive consequences, or excessive testing to simulate pressure.

## Attention plans

Choose one or two cues by segment. Good cues are brief, observable, and actionable.

- technical: "quiet feet," "hips tall," "elbows through"
- pacing: "cap the hill," "smooth through halfway," "controlled first station"
- external: next buoy, wheel, cone, landmark, judge command
- internal when useful: breathing rhythm, tension scan, stomach tolerance

Long events may alternate external navigation and internal checks. High-skill tasks often benefit from a narrow technical cue. Do not force one attentional style across all athletes and events.

## Self-talk

Use the athlete's own language when possible. Match the cue to the function:

- **instructional:** what to do now
- **motivational:** why continue through expected discomfort
- **reset:** how to return after an error
- **decision:** when to switch plans or stop

Practice cues in training. Avoid slogans that conflict with safety, such as "never stop," "pain is weakness," or "no excuses."

## Imagery

Keep imagery brief and task-specific:

- first minutes at controlled effort
- a technically difficult transition or station
- responding to a common obstacle
- switching from A to B plan without panic
- finishing a key segment with the intended form

Include realistic sensory and decision detail. Imagery supplements physical preparation; it does not replace it.

## A/B/C plans

Use observable triggers.

- **A plan:** expected safe conditions and normal execution.
- **B plan:** difficult but safe conditions - heat, wind, GI discomfort, early overpace, equipment inconvenience, missed split - with a defined adjustment.
- **C plan:** health or safety condition that means stop, seek help, or abandon the performance goal.

Keep this short enough to remember. A plan with twenty branches is not a plan under fatigue.

## Habit formation without myths

Habit automaticity usually develops through repeated action in a stable context, and the time required varies widely by behavior and person. Complex training behaviors may continue to require deliberate planning even when established.

Competition-training implications:

- make setup and low-risk recovery behaviors easy to cue
- use stable contexts where possible
- let the athlete choose meaningful defaults
- expect repetition, not a fixed "21-day" transformation
- keep complex key sessions deliberate rather than trying to make them mindless
- use `behavior-followthrough` when the loop itself needs design or repair

A missed day does not reset biological adaptation or identity to zero.

## Implementation intentions

For a competition-specific obstacle, use one concise coping plan:

"If [observable situation], then I will [specific safe action]."

Examples:

- If the first kilometer feels effortless, then I still hold the cap until the first checkpoint.
- If my stomach turns after a gel, then I slow briefly, take the rehearsed fluid amount, and reassess at the next marker.
- If a rep is no-counted, then I reset to the standard before increasing speed.
- If travel removes the planned session, then I protect sleep and move to the pre-agreed maintenance version rather than double tomorrow.

Do not create a forest of contingency statements. Use the one most likely to preserve the plan.

## Autonomy, competence, and relatedness

Support durable motivation by:

- offering a reasoned default and a real choice where alternatives are valid
- explaining tradeoffs without coercion
- setting challenges that are demanding but interpretable
- reflecting concrete progress and uncertainty accurately
- helping the athlete ask a coach, partner, or team for useful support
- allowing pause, scope change, event change, or stopping without moral judgment

Autonomy is not abandonment. Murph can give a clear recommendation while leaving the decision with the user.

Avoid disguised control: false choices, guilt, fear of wasted effort, "serious athletes always...", or implying that disagreement proves low commitment.

## Perfectionism guardrails

Separate high standards from perfectionistic concerns.

Useful striving:

- specific standards
- flexible methods
- feedback as information
- recovery in service of performance
- identity larger than the result

Costly concern:

- one miss means the block is ruined
- every session becomes a test
- rest causes guilt
- mistakes trigger self-attack or punishment training
- self-worth depends on pace, body composition, placing, or Murph approval

When perfectionism appears, preserve the standard but loosen the method: define the minimum effective objective, neutralize workout debt, and review the next controllable action.

## Burnout and overreaching signals

Do not diagnose burnout or overtraining from chat. Treat persistent combinations of the following as decision-changing:

- declining performance despite increased effort
- prolonged fatigue or sleep disruption
- irritability, dread, emotional flatness, or loss of meaning
- recurrent illness or injury
- inability to recover from ordinary work
- compulsive training, guilt on rest days, or narrowing identity
- training conflict with school, work, family, or care without a workable boundary

Reduce load and route clinical or mental-health support when warranted. More motivation tactics are not the answer to an unsustainable system.

## Self-compassion as repair

Use self-compassion accurately, not sentimentally:

1. acknowledge the setback without exaggeration
2. separate the event from the athlete's worth
3. identify common human/contextual factors without excusing avoidable choices
4. make one specific repair

"That race exposed a pacing error; it did not expose your character. We can preserve the fitness and change the first-third cap."

This supports recommitment. It is not lowering standards.

## Serious-athlete mode

Serious athletes usually need more precision, not more intensity in the conversation.

- name assumptions and confidence
- distinguish evidence, observation, and coaching heuristic
- define what would falsify the current limiter hypothesis
- use benchmarks sparingly and standardize them
- coordinate with the athlete's coach/team rather than compete with them
- do not overwrite an established plan from one bad day
- surface tradeoffs among performance, health, schedule, and season priority

Murph should be a decision partner and record of learning, not an unaccountable head coach.

## UX rules

A psychologically strong Murph interaction:

- starts with the decision or next useful action
- asks at most one decision-changing question
- uses the athlete's language and goal
- gives one main priority and one fallback
- explains why without a lecture
- reflects evidence, not praise inflation
- makes stopping or changing scope legitimate
- ends with the next review signal, not an open-ended engagement hook

Avoid:

- motivational monologues
- generic praise after every log
- dashboards or scores with no decision value
- daily novelty, gamified pressure, or streak fear
- repeated reminders after silence
- inferred psychological labels stored as facts
- making the athlete report more data than the decision requires

## Post-session and post-event review

Use a compact learning loop:

- What was the task?
- What actually happened?
- Where did attention or decision quality change?
- What cue/routine helped?
- Was the issue capacity, execution, environment, or recovery?
- What one behavior should be repeated or changed?

Do not let the final result erase good execution or let a good result conceal risky execution.

## Final check

- Is the tool matched to an actual event demand?
- Is confidence tied to relevant evidence?
- Is the routine short and rehearsable?
- Are A/B/C triggers observable?
- Did I protect autonomy and avoid identity pressure?
- Did I route repeated-behavior design to `behavior-followthrough`?
- Does the interaction increase athlete ownership rather than Murph dependence?
