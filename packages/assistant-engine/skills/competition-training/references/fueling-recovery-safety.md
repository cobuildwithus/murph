# Fueling, recovery, and safety

Use this reference when detailed nutrition, hydration, heat, altitude, sleep, illness, low energy availability, supplements, or safety-sensitive advice changes the plan.

This is general competition-preparation guidance, not medical clearance, diagnosis, or individualized treatment. Use a sports dietitian or clinician when the medical context, stakes, symptoms, eating history, or performance level requires it.

## Decision order

1. Is there an urgent or decision-changing health concern?
2. Is the athlete eating and recovering enough to support the work?
3. Does the event duration/environment require a fueling or hydration plan?
4. Has that plan been practiced under representative conditions?
5. What is the smallest change that improves execution without adding risk or burden?

Do not optimize supplements while the foundation or safety problem is unresolved.

## Energy availability first

Training adaptation requires enough energy to support exercise and normal physiology. Low energy availability can be intentional or unintentional and can affect athletes of any sex, body size, age, or performance level.

Decision-changing patterns include:

- rapid or continuing weight loss
- persistent fatigue or declining performance
- recurrent illness or injury
- menstrual disruption, low libido, or other hormonal changes
- recurrent bone stress or delayed healing
- persistent cold intolerance, mood change, or poor concentration
- restrictive eating, fear around food, compensatory exercise, or escalating body checking
- inability to recover despite reducing training stress

Do not diagnose REDs or an eating disorder from chat. Name the pattern, reduce performance pressure, avoid further restriction, and recommend qualified multidisciplinary assessment where appropriate.

Never prescribe weight loss, calorie restriction, dehydration, or body-composition manipulation as the default route to competition performance. Do not praise low body weight as proof of fitness.

## Everyday training nutrition

Keep guidance proportional to the question.

- Match total food and carbohydrate availability to training duration, intensity, and phase.
- Include adequate protein across the day to support repair and adaptation.
- Include fats, micronutrient-rich foods, and enough overall variety.
- Increase convenience and planning before increasing tracking burden.
- Use normal food as the default; products are tools for logistics and tolerance.
- Avoid turning every meal into a score or making calorie/macro tracking a prerequisite.

When the user reports low appetite around heavy training, discuss timing, energy-dense familiar options, liquids when tolerated, and professional help rather than simply demanding larger meals.

## Before training or competition

The goal is familiar, tolerated fuel and fluid without unnecessary GI load.

Consider:

- time since the last meal
- event duration and intensity
- early-morning or late-day timing
- heat and access to fluid
- GI history
- whether the user needs to start well fueled versus deliberately training a specific strategy under professional oversight

Use a familiar carbohydrate-rich meal or snack in a quantity the athlete has tolerated. Lower fiber, fat, or protein close to the start only when those have caused symptoms; do not impose a universal restriction.

Do not introduce a new carb-load, fasting strategy, supplement, or large meal on race morning.

## During-exercise carbohydrate

Use duration as a starting point, then adjust for intensity, tolerance, body size, aid access, and medical context.

- **Shorter than roughly 45-60 minutes:** fuel is often unnecessary for performance, though a pre-event intake or mouth rinse may matter in selected high-intensity contexts.
- **Roughly 1-2.5 hours:** a common starting range is about 30-60 g carbohydrate per hour.
- **Longer than roughly 2.5 hours:** some athletes benefit from up to about 90 g per hour using practiced mixtures and delivery formats.

These are orientation ranges, not quotas. A smaller tolerated amount can be better than a theoretical target that causes GI distress. Very high intakes require progressive gut training and, for serious athletes, qualified nutrition support.

Convert the plan into practical units the athlete has rehearsed: bottles, gels, chews, drink mix, or food portions. Include timing and access, not only grams.

## Gut training

GI tolerance is trainable for many athletes but variable.

Progress by changing one main element at a time:

- total carbohydrate per hour
- fluid concentration and volume
- carbohydrate type/mixture
- solid versus liquid format
- intake frequency
- exercise intensity, heat, or jostling

Practice on selected event-specific sessions, not every easy session and not first on race day. Record symptoms, context, and what changed. Use `self-management-experiments` when a bounded test would improve attribution.

Persistent, severe, bloody, or medically complex GI symptoms require clinical evaluation.

## Hydration

Both dehydration and overdrinking can be harmful. The goal is an individualized plan that limits excessive fluid loss without producing net overhydration.

Use:

- thirst and access
- prior body-mass change only when measured sensibly and interpreted cautiously
- sweat rate and salt-loss clues
- heat, humidity, clothing, altitude, duration, pace, and body tolerance
- urine and body-mass data as context, not a command to force fluid

Do not tell an athlete to drink as much as possible or to hit a generic large volume regardless of thirst. Avoid weight gain during endurance exercise from excess fluid.

### Sodium

Sodium needs vary substantially. It may help replace losses and support fluid intake in long/hot events, but it does not make forced overdrinking safe and does not prevent every cramp.

Base a starting plan on event duration, heat, sweat/salt history, available products, and tolerance. Avoid salt loading or very high-dose strategies without qualified guidance.

### Possible exercise-associated hyponatremia

Headache, nausea/vomiting, confusion, altered behavior, seizure, or collapse during or after prolonged exercise - especially with high fluid intake or weight gain - requires urgent medical evaluation. Do not advise more plain water while awaiting help.

## Race fueling plan

A useful plan states:

- familiar pre-event meal/snack and timing
- start-time contingency
- carbohydrate source and practical units per segment/hour
- fluid strategy and aid access
- sodium only when relevant
- caffeine only when rehearsed and appropriate
- B plan for heat, GI symptoms, missed aid, or dropped product
- C plan for symptoms requiring slowing, stopping, or medical help

Keep it memorable. Do not bury the plan in a nutrition lecture.

## Caffeine

Caffeine can help some athletes and impair others through anxiety, GI symptoms, heart-rate sensations, tremor, urinary urgency, or sleep disruption.

- use the lowest rehearsed amount that provides a benefit
- consider habitual use, body size, timing, event duration, medication/medical context, age, pregnancy, and anti-doping/product risk
- test away from the priority event
- protect sleep, especially with later events or multi-day competition
- do not escalate because a normal dose "stopped feeling strong"

Do not recommend caffeine to minors or medically complex users without appropriate professional/contextual guidance.

## Supplements

Few supplements have strong evidence for specific uses, and product contamination, dose error, medication interactions, cost, and anti-doping consequences matter.

- use food, training, sleep, and execution first
- verify governing-body and anti-doping rules
- prefer independently tested products when a qualified professional and athlete choose a supplement
- avoid stacks and last-minute experimentation
- never imply that supplements are required to be serious or elite

Route medication and interaction questions to a pharmacist/clinician.

## Recovery

Recovery is part of the dose, not a reward for earning it.

### Sleep

Athlete sleep needs vary by age, training, travel, and individual response. Protect sufficient opportunity and regularity rather than treating one universal number or wearable score as a diagnosis.

Decision-changing issues include:

- repeated short sleep around key work
- insomnia symptoms, breathing concerns, restless legs, or severe daytime sleepiness
- late caffeine or competition schedules
- travel and time-zone shifts
- anxiety-driven checking of sleep data

Use practical changes that fit the event and life context. Persistent sleep disorder symptoms need clinical evaluation.

### Rest and easy days

Easy days must be low enough to support the next priority. Complete rest may be appropriate. Avoid filling rest days with compensatory conditioning because the athlete feels guilty.

### Between-event recovery

For repeated heats or multi-day events, plan:

- immediate fluid and carbohydrate access as tolerated
- protein and a normal meal when timing allows
- cooling/warming and dry clothing
- feet/skin/equipment care
- a short down-regulation period
- the next warm-up start time
- sleep and transport logistics

Do not add aggressive recovery gadgets when food, fluid, temperature, and time are unresolved.

### Soreness and recovery products

Soreness is not a direct measure of adaptation. Massage, compression, cold, or other modalities may alter perception and can be useful by preference, but they do not replace appropriate load, sleep, and nutrition. Consider whether a modality could blunt a desired adaptation when used chronically.

## Heat

Heat adds physiological load and can invalidate ordinary pace/power targets.

### Preparation

- build progressive heat exposure when appropriate and safe, often over roughly one to two weeks rather than one heroic session
- reduce intensity/duration initially
- practice clothing, cooling, fluid access, and pacing
- check current event weather and organizer guidance close to the date
- use conservative targets when conditions exceed preparation

Athletes with prior heat illness, relevant medical conditions/medications, or limited thermoregulation need individualized professional guidance.

### Exertional heat illness

Altered mental status, collapse, severe confusion, seizure, or marked neurologic change in the heat is an emergency. Activate emergency care and begin rapid cooling when trained/equipped to do so; do not wait for a chat response.

Stop exercise and seek assessment for concerning escalating symptoms such as severe weakness, dizziness, vomiting, loss of coordination, or inability to cool.

## Cold, water, altitude, and remoteness

### Cold/wet

Plan layers, dry clothing, hand/foot protection, wind exposure, fueling access, and post-event warming. Cold water and wet conditions can impair dexterity and judgment before the athlete recognizes the decline.

### Open water

Do not recommend solo open-water practice. Use appropriate supervision, visibility, local knowledge, water-quality/temperature information, and event-specific safety rules. Pool fitness does not prove open-water competence.

### Altitude

Altitude response varies. Reduce early intensity expectations, allow acclimatization where possible, and consider sleep, hydration, iron status, travel, and medical history. Severe headache, confusion, ataxia, breathlessness at rest, or deterioration requires descent and urgent care.

### Remote or self-supported events

Add mandatory kit, navigation, communication, resupply, emergency contacts, turnaround criteria, and realistic rescue access. Fitness is only one part of readiness.

## Youth and developmental athletes

Do not apply adult training, fueling, weight-making, caffeine, or supplement assumptions by simple body-size scaling. Growth and maturation, total energy needs, school load, sleep, age-group rules, safeguarding, and qualified adult supervision can change the plan.

Keep the athlete's own goals and consent visible. Avoid year-round result pressure, early specialization claims, rapid weight change, supplement promotion, and unsupervised high-risk or maximal practice. Recurrent pain, growth concerns, menstrual disruption, restrictive eating, repeated illness, or marked fatigue warrants qualified pediatric or sports-health input.

## Masters and older athletes

Do not infer capacity from age alone. Use recent training, health history, medications, symptoms, recovery response, and event demands. Preserve strength and power work when appropriate, allow recovery to be individualized rather than automatically prolonged, and route new cardiopulmonary, neurologic, metabolic, renal, or musculoskeletal concerns for appropriate evaluation.

## Adaptive and para athletes

Center the athlete's expertise and actual classification/equipment demands. Relevant considerations may include skin and pressure care, transfers, prosthetic or chair fit, thermoregulation, autonomic function, medication, accessibility, and impairment-specific health history. These vary widely; do not infer them from a diagnosis label or treat disability as illness.

Use the current organizer rules and athlete-selected coach/clinician support when classification, equipment, health, or safety changes the recommendation.

## Illness and return to training

Do not diagnose illness or apply a simplistic "above the neck" rule as clearance.

Pause hard training with fever, systemic symptoms, chest symptoms, significant breathing difficulty, dehydration, or marked functional decline. Resume in stages after daily function and easy exercise normalize, following clinician guidance when relevant.

Do not make up missed training. The first goal is to confirm normal response, not recover lost fitness in one session.

Chest pain/pressure, fainting, severe breathlessness, concerning palpitations, blue/gray color, or persistent worsening requires urgent medical evaluation.

## Pain, injury, and bone stress

Use `physical-therapy` for new or materially changed musculoskeletal pain, weakness, altered gait, or loss of function before designing around it.

Urgent or prompt evaluation is warranted for:

- major trauma, deformity, rapidly increasing swelling, or inability to bear weight
- neurologic weakness/numbness or bowel/bladder changes
- severe focal bone pain, pain at rest/night, or recurrent bone-stress history
- worsening pain that changes movement or normal daily function

Do not use pain tolerance as evidence of readiness. Do not prescribe analgesics to enable training through an unresolved injury.

## Rhabdomyolysis concern

Severe muscle pain/swelling/weakness with dark urine after extreme or unfamiliar exertion can be an emergency. Stop exercise and seek urgent care. Do not advise "flushing it out" with forced water at home.

## Menstrual-cycle and hormonal considerations

Average performance effects across menstrual-cycle phases are small and highly variable. Do not impose a rigid cycle-based plan on every athlete.

Use the athlete's repeated symptoms and preferences when they meaningfully change training. New menstrual disruption, persistent symptoms, pregnancy possibility, or other hormonal concerns may require medical assessment, especially alongside underfueling or bone stress.

## Pregnancy and postpartum

Competition goals during pregnancy or postpartum require individualized clinical guidance, current symptoms, prior training, pelvic-floor/abdominal considerations, delivery/recovery history, feeding/sleep demands, and event risk. Do not treat prior fitness as blanket clearance.

Urgent symptoms such as heavy bleeding, chest pain, fainting, severe headache, calf swelling/pain, or severe shortness of breath require medical care.

## Cramping

Exercise-associated cramping is multifactorial. Immediate management usually includes reducing/stopping the provoking effort and gentle lengthening of the affected muscle when safe.

Do not promise that salt alone prevents cramps. Review pacing, fatigue, heat, prior cramp history, training specificity, fluid/fuel plan, and medical context.

## Body composition and weigh-ins

When a sport has weight classes or weigh-ins, do not design rapid cuts, dehydration, purging, laxative/diuretic use, sauna protocols, or extreme restriction. These can impair health and performance and require qualified sport-specific medical/nutrition oversight.

Use competition-valid strength/skill and health as the primary performance evidence, not leanness.

## Mental health and exercise compulsion

Route qualified support when the user reports self-harm thoughts, severe depression/anxiety, panic, eating-disorder behavior, or exercise they feel unable to stop despite harm.

Do not use the event as leverage to keep someone training through mental-health risk. Preserving the person outranks preserving the plan.

## Emergency signs

Direct emergency help rather than continuing the training conversation for:

- chest pressure/pain with exertion, fainting, or near-fainting
- severe or unusual breathing difficulty, blue/gray color, or rapidly worsening respiratory symptoms
- new one-sided weakness, confusion, seizure, or acute neurologic signs
- altered mental status or collapse in heat
- severe allergic reaction
- major trauma, obvious deformity, or inability to bear weight after injury
- dark urine with severe muscle pain/weakness after extreme exertion
- suspected severe hyponatremia, altitude illness, or drowning-related emergency
- any situation the user or bystanders believe is an emergency

Use local emergency services and on-site medical personnel. Do not delay for more data.

## Output patterns

### Fueling answer

- event duration/intensity/environment
- current practiced intake and symptoms
- one starting plan in practical units
- rehearsal session
- B plan for common failure
- referral/stop rule when relevant

### Recovery answer

- likely limiting recovery factor
- one immediate change
- one training-load adjustment
- what to monitor
- review timing and escalation rule

### Safety answer

- concern and why it changes training
- what to stop or modify now
- appropriate level of care
- what evidence would allow planning to resume

## Final check

- Is this within general guidance rather than diagnosis or treatment?
- Did I address energy availability before marginal optimization?
- Is the fueling/hydration plan individualized and rehearsed?
- Did I avoid forced drinking, rapid weight loss, and supplement hype?
- Are environmental and equipment risks represented?
- Are urgent and non-urgent routes clear?
- Is the answer practical without creating unnecessary tracking burden?
