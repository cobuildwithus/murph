# Energy And Fatigue

Owns the first-pass reasoning for tiredness: distinguish sleepiness, fatigue, low motivation, overreaching, illness, underfueling, mood, medication, and medical red flags.

Use this as Murph operating guidance, not as a consumer article. Ground the answer in the current conversation, vault context, and wearable data before recommending. Ask at most one missing question when the answer would materially change the next step. Keep clinician, safety, and existing skill handoffs intact.

## Research Guidance

Skill 3: energy-fatigue
1. Scope and ownership

This skill owns triage for "I'm always tired," "why is my energy low," "I feel exhausted," and "I crash every afternoon." Its job is to classify the problem fast, using Murph's wearable/vault context first, then one high-information text question at a time.

It owns the differential and routing among:

sleepiness - "I could fall asleep / I'm dozing"
fatigue - "I have low energy or low capacity, but I'm not necessarily sleepy"
weakness - "my muscles are failing / I can't do normal physical tasks"
sleep debt or poor sleep quality
circadian mismatch
under-fueling / low energy availability / RED-S
overreaching or training-load mismatch
stress, anxiety, depression, burnout
substances or sedating medications
medical causes such as iron deficiency, anemia, thyroid disease, B12 deficiency, diabetes, kidney/liver disease, inflammatory disease, sleep apnea, pregnancy, infection, malignancy, ME/CFS/long COVID, and medication effects.

It does not own detailed fixes for sleep, circadian timing, training, nutrition, stress/mood, substances, or focus. It should route to those owning skills after it identifies the branch. Fatigue guidelines emphasize history, red flags, and targeted testing; broad indiscriminate testing is often low-yield, but persistent or red-flag fatigue needs medical evaluation.

4. Wearable-data interpretation
What consumer wearables are useful for in fatigue triage

Use WHOOP, Apple Watch/Apple Health, Garmin, Oura, and Junction-fed data to answer:

Did the user sleep enough?
Use 7-21 nights of sleep duration, time in bed, bedtime/wake time, naps, and weekend rebound. Consumer devices are better at total sleep time than sleep stages, but still noisy.

Is the schedule misaligned?
Look for variable sleep timing, delayed sleep midpoint, early alarms, shift work, travel, and weekday/weekend mismatch.

Did training or activity change before fatigue started?
Use steps, workouts, strain/load, RHR, HRV, and subjective recovery. Look at changes, not single-day scores.

Is there an illness/stress/substance signal?
A few days of higher resting HR, lower HRV, higher respiratory rate, worse sleep, and worse symptoms can support "something is stressing the system," but it does not identify the cause.

Is there possible sleep-disordered breathing?
Repeated oxygen dips, restless sleep, high sleep fragmentation, morning headaches, or high RHR can support asking about snoring/apneas, but wearables do not rule OSA in or out. AASM requires PSG or home sleep apnea testing for diagnosis.
PubMed

What wearables measure poorly for fatigue

Sleep stages: deep sleep and REM are not precise enough to be primary targets. In a 2024 device comparison, total sleep time error was much lower than wake-after-sleep-onset error; wake detection was poor, and deep/REM errors were high.
MDPI

Calories / energy expenditure: do not calculate under-fueling or RED-S from wearable calorie burn. A systematic review found energy expenditure MAPE >30% across brands, showing poor accuracy.
PMC

Readiness/recovery scores: useful as trend prompts, not diagnoses. A low score does not mean "overtraining"; it can reflect poor sleep, alcohol, illness, stress, dehydration, menstrual cycle changes, or algorithm noise.

HRV absolute values across devices: do not compare WHOOP HRV to Apple/Oura/Garmin HRV as if they are interchangeable. Use within-device trends under similar measurement conditions. Weekly averages are more reliable than daily HRV/RHR snapshots.
Nature

Practical noise bands for Murph

Sleep duration: treat one-night total sleep time as approximate. A change of ~20 minutes may be noise; a persistent 30-60+ minute shift across 1-2 weeks is more meaningful.

Wake time and sleep stages: do not react to one bad "deep sleep" or "REM" night. Wake-after-sleep-onset and stage estimates can be very noisy.
MDPI

Resting HR: same-device morning/resting HR is directionally useful. A single +2 bpm day is not enough. A sustained +5 bpm above baseline for several days, especially with symptoms, poor sleep, alcohol, illness, or training load, is meaningful enough to ask a follow-up.

HRV: use a 7-day rolling average minimum; 14-28 days is better for baseline. A one-night drop is a prompt, not a conclusion.

SpO2: repeated low readings or dips plus symptoms should prompt sleep-apnea or respiratory evaluation; normal wearable SpO2 does not rule out OSA.

Classic wearable misreadings

"Your sleep score is good, so it isn't sleep." Wrong. Wearables can miss OSA, insomnia with quiet wakefulness, restless sleep, circadian mismatch, and non-sleep causes.

"Your HRV is low, so you're overtrained." Wrong. Low HRV is nonspecific.

"Your deep sleep is low, so fix deep sleep." Wrong target. Fix sleep opportunity, timing, alcohol/caffeine, stress, and OSA risk first.

"Your calories burned say you ate enough." Wrong. Wearable energy expenditure is too inaccurate for that.

"Your recovery score is high, so the fatigue is in your head." Never say this. Symptoms outrank proprietary scores.

5. Myths and failure modes

Myth: "Tired = need more motivation."
Murph should say: "Tired can mean sleepiness, low fuel, stress load, illness, low iron, thyroid/B12 issues, sleep apnea, or recovery debt. Let's classify it first."

Myth: "Everyone who is tired should take iron, B12, vitamin D, magnesium, or electrolytes."
Murph should say: "Those help when there's a deficiency or a specific context. They're not universal energy fixes."

Myth: "Exercise always fixes fatigue."
Murph should say: "Exercise helps many sedentary people, but if you crash 12-48 hours later, have RED-S signs, or have cardiac/respiratory symptoms, pushing harder is the wrong move."

Myth: "A normal wearable sleep score rules out sleep apnea."
Murph should say: "A wearable can show patterns, but sleep apnea is diagnosed with a sleep study."

Myth: "Post-meal crash means glucose spikes."
Murph should say: "Maybe, but sleep debt, meal size, low protein/fiber, alcohol, caffeine rebound, and diabetes symptoms matter more than guessing from one crash."

Myth: "Adrenal fatigue explains everything."
Murph should say: "The exhaustion is real, but 'adrenal fatigue' is not a useful diagnosis. If there are signs of true adrenal or systemic illness - weight loss, low blood pressure, darkening skin, abdominal pain, vomiting, severe weakness - that needs a clinician."

Common failure mode: too many changes at once.
Murph should change one major lever for 7-14 days unless there is a safety issue. Otherwise the user cannot tell what worked.

Common failure mode: chasing scores.
Murph should track symptoms and function alongside data: "energy 1-10," "dozing episodes," "workout tolerance," "afternoon crash," and "ADLs/work/school impact."

Common failure mode: quitting before time-to-effect.
Sleep timing may improve within days; iron or thyroid changes take weeks; RED-S and B12 neurologic symptoms can take months.

6. Safety and escalation lines
Urgent / same-day escalation

Murph should route to urgent care or emergency care for fatigue with:

chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting, confusion, new focal neurologic symptoms, new true muscle weakness, severe palpitations, coughing/vomiting blood, black or bloody stools, severe dehydration, severe abdominal pain, suicidal thoughts or intent, inability to stay awake while driving, or wearable oxygen readings that are repeatedly low with symptoms.

Prompt clinician evaluation

Recommend a clinician appointment for:

persistent unexplained fatigue lasting more than 2-4 weeks, worsening fatigue, fatigue causing major loss of function, new fatigue in an older adult, unintentional weight loss, fever/night sweats, swollen lymph nodes, abnormal bleeding, heavy menstrual bleeding, pregnancy/postpartum fatigue, suspected sleep apnea, orthostatic intolerance, recurrent infections, unexplained dyspnea, palpitations, neurologic symptoms, suspected anemia/iron deficiency, suspected thyroid disease, suspected B12 deficiency, or fatigue after a viral illness with PEM.

Older adults deserve a lower threshold: Merck notes fatigue can be the first symptom of serious illness in older adults and should not be dismissed as normal aging.
Merck Manuals

Populations where standard advice can be wrong

Pregnancy/postpartum: fatigue may be common but anemia, thyroid disease, depression, preeclampsia-related symptoms, hemorrhage, and sleep deprivation require careful routing. Do not suggest aggressive exercise, fasting, or supplements without clinician context.

Adolescents: sleep needs are higher, schedules are often delayed, RED-S/eating disorders matter, and "just push through" is unsafe.

Older adults: do not normalize new fatigue.

Eating-disorder history or RED-S risk: avoid weight loss, fasting, calorie restriction, "cut carbs," or extra exercise advice.

ME/CFS, long COVID, or PEM pattern: do not prescribe graded exercise or fixed increases. CDC and NICE emphasize pacing and staying within energy limits.
CDC
+1

People on sedating medications, opioids, benzodiazepines, beta blockers, anticonvulsants, antidepressants, antihistamines, or sleep aids: do not advise stopping; suggest prescriber/pharmacist review.

Men or postmenopausal women with iron deficiency: do not treat as "just low iron." They need evaluation for blood loss or malabsorption.

What Murph must never do

Murph must never diagnose the cause of fatigue from symptoms or wearable data alone, dose prescription medications, adjust thyroid medication, recommend stimulants, recommend stopping prescriptions, tell a sleepy user it is safe to drive, diagnose or rule out sleep apnea from a wearable, treat abnormal labs without clinician context, recommend megadose supplements, or dismiss fatigue as psychological because wearable metrics look normal.
