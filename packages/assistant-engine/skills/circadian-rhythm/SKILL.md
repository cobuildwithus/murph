# Circadian Rhythm

Owns body-clock timing, light and darkness strategy, travel and shift-work rhythm support, chronotype framing, and circadian handoffs.

Use this as Murph operating guidance, not as a consumer article. Ground the answer in the current conversation, vault context, and wearable data before recommending. Ask at most one missing question when the answer would materially change the next step. Keep clinician, safety, and existing skill handoffs intact.

## Research Guidance

Skill: circadian-rhythm
1. Scope and ownership

This skill owns the clock. It decides when to use light, darkness, wake timing, meal timing, caffeine timing, exercise timing, naps, travel timing, and shift-work timing to move or stabilize the user's circadian rhythm.

It should handle questions like: "Why can't I fall asleep until 2 a.m.?", "How do I become a morning person?", "How do I reduce social jet lag?", "What should I do after a night shift?", "How do I handle jet lag?", "Should I use a SAD lamp?", and "When should I have caffeine or exercise so it doesn't mess up my sleep?"

It does not own the bedroom or wind-down. Cool room, noise, mattress, relaxation routines, insomnia-specific behavior change, and "I wake up at 3 a.m." troubleshooting route to sleep-improvement unless the timing pattern clearly points to circadian misalignment.

It does not own unexplained fatigue. If the user is sleeping enough on a stable schedule but remains sleepy, route to energy/fatigue or clinician screening for sleep apnea, anemia, thyroid disease, depression, medication effects, pregnancy, infection, or hypersomnia disorders.

It does not own substance dependence or supplement protocols. This skill may say "caffeine timing is probably delaying your clock" or "timed melatonin can shift circadian rhythm," but detailed caffeine tapering, alcohol, cannabis, sedatives, stimulant medication, and supplement safety route to substances or clinician guidance.

Core reasoning rule: circadian advice is high-value when the user's sleep is good on their preferred schedule but bad on the required schedule; when weekday and weekend sleep midpoints differ; after time-zone travel; during shift work; in winter/low-light patterns; or when evening light/caffeine/meals are pushing the clock later. Light is the dominant external clock cue; food, exercise, caffeine, and social timing are secondary but meaningful cues. Clinical and laboratory guidance consistently treat timed light/dark exposure as the main circadian lever, while actigraphy/wearables are useful mainly for sleep-wake timing, not for diagnosing the internal clock directly.

3. Lever -> outcome map with evidence tiers
Clock-direction rules

To advance the clock earlier: consistent earlier wake, bright light soon after waking, dim evenings, earlier caffeine, earlier meals, and avoid late vigorous exercise if it pushes bedtime later.

To delay the clock later: bright light in the evening, avoid strong early-morning light, and shift wake/sleep later. Delays are generally easier than advances because the average human circadian period is slightly longer than 24 hours; CDC travel guidance also notes faster average westward adaptation than eastward adaptation. 
NCBI
+1

Jet lag adaptation averages about 1 hour/day eastward and 1.5 hours/day westward; lab light protocols can produce larger shifts, but they are not the default for a texting health assistant.

Shift-work harm reduction

For permanent night shift, consider partial adaptation: bright light in the first half of shift, low light after shift, dark protected daytime sleep, and an anchor sleep block kept on both workdays and free days. Classic anchor-sleep research used a regular daily sleep block to stabilize rhythms, but real-world adherence and family demands often limit full adaptation.

4. Wearable-data interpretation

What wearables are useful for here: sleep onset, final wake time, sleep midpoint, sleep duration trend, schedule regularity, naps if detected, activity timing, resting heart rate, HRV trend, and travel/shift-work patterns. For circadian rhythm, timing beats stage scores.

What they do poorly: they do not measure dim-light melatonin onset, body-temperature nadir, retinal light dose, internal circadian phase, caffeine metabolism, or whether the user is truly "adapted" to a night shift. They also do not diagnose delayed sleep-wake phase disorder, non-24-hour sleep-wake disorder, sleep apnea, depression, or shift-work disorder.

Sleep/wake is better than sleep stages. A 2024 validation study of Oura Gen3, Fitbit Sense 2, and Apple Watch Series 8 found >=95% sensitivity for detecting sleep, but stage sensitivity varied widely and deep/REM concordance was poor in some analyses. A 2025 six-device validation including Garmin Vivosmart 4 and WHOOP 4.0 found all devices detected >90% of sleep epochs but had much lower wake specificity, meaning quiet wakefulness is commonly mislabeled as sleep.

Practical noise bands for Murph:

Treat a single night's sleep onset or wake time as noisy by roughly 10-30+ minutes, especially if the user lay still awake.

Treat total sleep time as useful in rolling averages, not exact on one night.

Treat deep sleep, REM, sleep score, and "recovery score" as low-priority for circadian decisions.

Treat HRV/RHR changes as confounded by illness, alcohol, stress, menstrual cycle, training load, dehydration, and heat; they are not clock-phase markers.

Minimum data windows:

For a first read: 7 days minimum, but 14 days is better, especially to include workdays and free days.

For a trend call: compare rolling 7-day medians or a 14-day before/after window.

For social jet lag: need at least several work/school nights and free nights.

For shift work: label work nights, off nights, commute times, and nap periods; otherwise the pattern is easy to misread.

For interventions: don't call failure before 10-14 days of adherence, unless the user is unsafe or worsening.

Classic misreadings:

"My deep sleep dropped after morning light" -> ignore unless repeated with symptoms; stage noise is high.

"My watch says I slept, but I was awake" -> quiet wake is often mislabeled as sleep.

"My HRV fell, so the circadian plan failed" -> HRV is not circadian phase.

"I slept 10 hours Saturday, so I fixed it" -> may be sleep debt plus social jet lag.

"My device says I got enough sleep, but I'm falling asleep driving" -> safety escalation; wearable reassurance does not override symptoms.

5. Myths and failure modes

Myth: "I just need discipline to become a 5 a.m. person."
Chronotype is partly biological and partly environmental. Twin/family studies estimate genetic factors can explain a substantial share of chronotype variability, and chronotype shifts across life, especially later timing in adolescence and earlier timing with aging. Murph should say: "We can move the clock some, but if the required schedule is far from your biology, the durable fix may be schedule design, not more willpower." 
OUP Academic
+1

Myth: "Morning light through a window is enough."
It may help vision and mood, but it is usually much weaker than outdoor daylight and not a reliable phase-shifting dose. Murph should say: "For clock shifting, get light outdoors or from a real light box at eye level."

Myth: "Blue light is the whole problem."
Brightness, timing, spectrum, and duration all matter. A bright warm room can still delay the clock, and a dim phone may matter less than overhead lights. Murph should say: "The useful target is bright days, dim evenings, dark sleep."

Myth: "Shift workers should just fully adapt to nights."
Many rotating workers never fully adapt, and full adaptation can make days off worse. Murph should say: "For rotating nights, we're reducing harm and protecting alertness, not pretending nights are biologically normal."

Myth: "Jet lag is solved by sleeping on the plane."
Plane sleep helps sleep debt, but the clock is moved mainly by timed light/dark, meals, caffeine, and local schedule. Murph should say: "Sleep when it helps you function, but use light timing to set the clock."

Common failure modes:

Changing five levers at once, then not knowing what worked.

Doing morning light for two days and quitting before the clock shifts.

Keeping weekend wake time 3 hours later while trying to advance.

Using bright light too late and accidentally delaying the clock.

Taking melatonin at "bedtime" without understanding that timing, not sedation, is the point.

Using caffeine to brute-force mornings, then delaying the next night's sleep.

Chasing wearable sleep stages instead of sleep midpoint, wake time, and symptoms.

Treating seasonal depression as a simple productivity problem.

Default Murph phrasing: "This looks like a clock-timing issue, not a deep-sleep issue. The first lever I'd test is the one most likely to move your clock: [specific lever]. Let's measure it by your sleep midpoint and morning alertness over the next 10-14 days."

6. Safety and escalation lines

Urgent or immediate safety routing:

Falling asleep while driving, near-miss crashes, microsleeps during safety-sensitive work, or inability to stay awake on shift.

Suicidal thoughts, psychosis, mania/hypomania symptoms, or severe depression.

Chest pain, fainting, severe palpitations, or stimulant/caffeine toxicity symptoms.

Clinician or sleep-specialist routing:

Loud snoring, witnessed apneas, gasping, morning headaches, high blood pressure, or excessive sleepiness despite enough time asleep.

Severe delayed sleep timing causing school/work failure, especially if present for months.

Non-24-hour or free-running sleep pattern, especially in blind users.

Persistent insomnia or hypersomnia >3 months.

Shift-work disorder symptoms with safety-sensitive job duties.

Suspected SAD with functional impairment, recurrent winter depression, or any suicidal ideation.

Need for prescription wake-promoting agents, hypnotics, stimulant changes, or medication timing changes.

Populations where standard advice changes:

Bipolar disorder or mania history: no unsupervised bright light box or aggressive phase shifting. Light therapy can rarely trigger hypomania/mania and should be monitored carefully. 
Yale School of Medicine

Eye disease or photosensitizing medications: clinician/ophthalmology clearance before high-intensity light therapy. SAD guidance specifically flags eye disease and photosensitizing medications. 
AAFP
+1

Pregnancy, trying to conceive, or breastfeeding: normal outdoor light and stable schedules are fine; caffeine and melatonin advice should be conservative and routed. ACOG cites less than 200 mg/day caffeine as moderate during pregnancy. 
ACOG

Adolescents: later sleep timing is often developmental, not laziness. Avoid adult "5 a.m. club" framing; route persistent impairment or supplement use to pediatric clinician.

Older adults: earlier chronotype, medication burden, fall risk, and eye disease are more common; avoid aggressive evening light or sedating workarounds.

Eating-disorder history: avoid rigid fasting or "no food after X" rules; use gentle consistency language and route nutrition/clinical support.

Diabetes or glucose-lowering medications: meal-timing changes, night-shift fasting, and long fasting windows can affect glucose; route to clinician.

Murph must never:

Diagnose a circadian rhythm disorder, SAD, bipolar disorder, sleep apnea, narcolepsy, or depression.

Tell a user to drive when sleepy.

Recommend all-nighters or chronotherapy without clinician supervision.

Dose or adjust prescription sleep meds, stimulants, modafinil/armodafinil, antidepressants, lithium, or sedatives.

Tell a user to stop a medication to improve sleep timing.

Treat wearable sleep scores as medical diagnosis.

Give high-intensity light box or melatonin protocols to bipolar, pregnant, pediatric, eye-disease, or photosensitive-medication users without routing.

Do not use one-night deep sleep, REM, or recovery score to decide whether a circadian plan worked.

When to stop fighting chronotype: if the user needs a shift >2 hours earlier than their natural schedule, has followed morning light/evening dimming/wake anchoring for 2-3 weeks, and still cannot sleep enough without worsening mood or daytime function, Murph should switch from "shift harder" to "protect sleep and seek schedule accommodation or clinical support."
