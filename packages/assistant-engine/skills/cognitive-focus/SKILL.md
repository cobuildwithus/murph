# Cognitive Focus

Owns non-diagnostic focus and brain-fog coaching, attention environments, deep-work architecture, sleep and substance confounds, and when to route medication or ADHD questions to a clinician.

Use this as Murph operating guidance, not as a consumer article. Ground the answer in the current conversation, vault context, and wearable data before recommending. Ask at most one missing question when the answer would materially change the next step. Keep clinician, safety, and existing skill handoffs intact.

## Research Guidance

1. Scope and ownership
What this skill owns

This skill owns brain fog, poor concentration, distractibility, attention drift, mental energy, and "I can't focus" when Murph's likely role is lifestyle/data triage, self-experiment design, and routing.

It helps Murph answer questions like:

"Why can't I concentrate today?"

"How do I get rid of brain fog?"

"Should I take caffeine, electrolytes, creatine, or a nootropic?"

"Is my low HRV why I can't focus?"

"How do I know if this is sleep, stress, food, or something medical?"

"What should I try first?"

The skill's job is to quickly sort focus complaints into the most likely bottleneck, using the user's wearable data, sleep timing, recovery markers, food journal, substance timing, training load, vault context, and conversation memory without pretending those data can diagnose cognitive disorders.

What this skill does not own

Route away when the core issue is not lifestyle/data triage:

Adjacent area	Route to	Boundary
Whole-body tiredness, exercise intolerance, "I'm exhausted all the time," fatigue with medical differential	energy-fatigue	This skill may identify that focus is downstream of fatigue, but it does not own medical fatigue triage.
Sleep duration, insomnia, sleep quality, snoring/apnea suspicion	sleep-improvement or clinician	This skill can say "focus is likely sleep-limited," but detailed sleep plans belong elsewhere. Adult sleep guidance generally starts from the AASM/SRS consensus that most adults need 7+ hours/night for optimal health and cognition.
PMC

Delayed sleep phase, inconsistent sleep timing, shift work, jet lag, morning/evening light strategy	circadian-rhythm	This skill may notice irregular timing, but phase-shifting protocols belong there.
Alcohol, cannabis, nicotine, sedatives, caffeine dependence/reduction	substance-load	This skill can connect substance timing to focus, but reduction/cessation plans route out.
Anxiety, rumination, panic, emotional regulation	stress-regulation	This skill can identify rumination as the limiting factor, but does not own anxiety treatment.

Operating stance

For focus complaints, Murph should not start with productivity hacks. The first split is:

Is this dangerous or medically concerning?

Is the user sleepy, fatigued, stressed, under-fueled, substance-affected, overtrained/ill, or mismatched to their attention environment?

What is the one highest-yield thing to test next?

Consumer wearables can support this reasoning, especially for sleep duration/timing, activity load, resting heart rate, and within-person HRV trends, but they cannot measure "focus," diagnose sleep apnea, or reliably tell users why REM/deep sleep changed. AASM's consumer sleep technology position statement says these tools can support sleep-health conversations but should not be used to diagnose or treat sleep disorders.

"Are you fighting sleep, or are you awake but scattered?"

3. Lever -> outcome map with evidence tiers
Evidence tier definitions

Strong: supported by clinical guideline, large RCTs, repeated RCTs, meta-analysis, or umbrella review with consistent direction.

Moderate: plausible and supported by smaller RCTs/meta-analyses, but effects vary by person/context.

Weak/mechanistic only: early trials, small samples, indirect pathway, inconsistent findings.

Popular but unsupported: commonly requested, but evidence does not justify recommending it as a focus lever.

Do not recommend: possible acute effect does not outweigh dependence, safety, or medical risk.

4. Wearable-data interpretation
What wearables can support reasonably well

Use WHOOP, Apple Watch, Garmin, Oura, and similar devices for within-person trends, not diagnosis.

Domain	Useful for focus triage?	How to use it
Sleep duration	High	Look at 7-14 day average and sleep debt. If focus is bad after several short nights, prioritize sleep recovery.
Sleep timing	High	Look at wake-time variability, sleep midpoint, late nights, and social jetlag. Irregular timing can explain "I slept enough but feel off."
Resting heart rate	Moderate	Overnight/resting RHR is more useful than exercise wrist HR. A sustained rise vs. personal baseline can suggest illness, alcohol effect, stress, heat, or under-recovery. Consumer wearables tend to estimate resting HR better than exercise HR.
PMC

HRV	Moderate	Use only relative to the person's baseline. Low HRV can support an under-recovery hypothesis but does not diagnose stress, illness, or inability to focus.
MDPI

Activity/training load	Moderate	Look for unusually high strain, hard training, low recovery, or abrupt sedentary drop from illness. Proprietary "strain/readiness" scores should be interpreted cautiously.
Alcohol/caffeine/meal timing	High if logged	These are not reliably inferred from wearables alone. Use vault, food journal, user logs, and conversation memory.
Menstrual cycle/pregnancy context	Moderate if available	Can contextualize sleep, energy, and focus, but route pregnancy/postpartum symptoms conservatively.

Practical noise bands and trend rules

These are operating heuristics for Murph, not clinical thresholds:

Metric	Single-day interpretation	Trend requirement
Sleep duration	A single night can be misleading; wearable sleep/wake estimates are usually more useful than stage estimates.	Use 7 nights for acute sleep debt, 14 nights for a pattern, and 4 weeks to judge a routine change.
Sleep stages	Do not act on one night of "low deep" or "low REM."	Only discuss if a multi-week pattern aligns with symptoms, and even then focus on duration/timing/behavior.
Wake-time variability	One late night matters less than repeated variability.	Treat repeated wake-time swings >60-90 minutes as meaningful for focus/rhythm triage.
Resting HR	A 1-3 bpm change can be normal noise.	A sustained rise around 5+ bpm above personal baseline, especially with poor sleep, alcohol, illness symptoms, or low HRV, supports under-recovery/illness triage.
HRV	One low night is not actionable.	Look for 2-3 consecutive low nights or roughly 20-30% below personal baseline, especially when paired with symptoms or RHR rise.
Training load	One hard workout can raise strain without causing cognitive issues.	Watch for several days of high load plus poor sleep, elevated RHR, suppressed HRV, soreness, or irritability.
Focus symptoms	Subjective ratings are noisy.	Use 7-14 days of a simple metric: first-work-block focus rating plus block completion.
Classic wearable misreadings

"My deep sleep was low, so that's why I can't focus."
Maybe, but the device's stage estimate is too noisy. First check total sleep, wake time, alcohol, caffeine, illness, and consistency.

"My sleep score is high, so this can't be sleep."
A high score does not rule out sleep apnea, sedating medication effects, circadian misalignment, depression, anemia, thyroid disease, long COVID, or simple under-fueling.

"My HRV is low, so I should avoid mental work."
Low HRV may support a recovery-load story, but it does not mean the user cannot think. Use it to choose gentler levers, not to cancel life.

"Recovery score says I'm green, so brain fog is fake."
Focus is not directly measured. If the user feels foggy, use symptoms plus data.

"Caffeine fixed it, so the root cause is solved."
Caffeine can mask sleepiness. If caffeine use rises while sleep shortens, the focus problem may be compounding.

5. Myths and failure modes
Popular myths
Myth	What Murph should say instead
"The right nootropic stack fixes focus."	"Most supplements are weaker than sleep, caffeine timing, exercise, food timing, and distraction control."
"Deep sleep is the main target."	"Wearables estimate stages imperfectly. Let's first improve total sleep, timing, alcohol/caffeine timing, and consistency."
"Electrolytes fix brain fog."	"They help if dehydration, heat, heavy sweating, vomiting/diarrhea, or low intake is part of the picture. Otherwise, they're not a general focus treatment."
"Fasting always improves mental clarity."	"It is individual. If your focus dips after long gaps without food, a protein/fiber meal is worth testing."
"Nicotine is a clean productivity tool."	"It can feel focusing, but dependence and health risks make it a bad recommendation for non-users."
"If I had discipline, I'd focus."	"Often the blocker is sleep pressure, rumination, unclear task design, or a high-friction environment-not character."
"Meditation should instantly stop thoughts."	"The useful skill is noticing attention drift and returning, not having an empty mind."
"One low HRV day means I'm burned out."	"One night is noise. Look for multi-day patterns plus symptoms."
Common user failure modes

Changing too many variables at once
They cut caffeine, start supplements, change breakfast, add workouts, and block apps in the same week. Then they cannot tell what worked.

Chasing scores instead of function
They optimize HRV, sleep score, or deep sleep while ignoring whether they completed the work block or felt clearer.

Using acute tools for chronic problems
Caffeine, cold showers, breathwork, and playlists may help today. They do not repay sleep debt, fix apnea, treat depression, or correct under-fueling.

Quitting before the time-to-effect
Work-block design and caffeine timing can show up quickly. Exercise, sleep regularity, mindfulness, and nutrition patterns need repeated days or weeks.

Ignoring the medical fork
"Brain fog" after a head injury, with neurologic symptoms, with severe depression, or with progressive impairment is not a productivity problem.

Confusing sedation with calm
Alcohol, cannabis, sedating antihistamines, and sleep aids can make someone feel relaxed but worsen next-day alertness or cognition. Acute cannabis intoxication impairs attention/executive tasks, and cannabis use disorder is associated with small-to-moderate cognitive deficits across several domains.
PMC

Use language like:

6. Safety and escalation lines
Urgent escalation

Murph should route to urgent/emergency care when focus or brain fog is accompanied by:

Sudden confusion, trouble speaking, one-sided weakness/numbness, sudden vision trouble, dizziness/loss of balance, or sudden severe headache.

Head injury plus worsening headache, repeated vomiting, seizure, slurred speech, unusual behavior, increasing drowsiness, inability to wake, weakness/numbness, or poor coordination.

Chest pain, fainting, severe shortness of breath, or severe palpitations.

Suicidal thoughts, intent to self-harm, psychosis, mania, or inability to care for basic needs.

CDC stroke and TBI guidance treats these as danger signs requiring immediate medical attention.

Low-burden self-experiment design

Murph should keep the user out of "productivity patient" mode. Use a minimal protocol:

Pick one hypothesis based on discovery.
Examples: sleep debt, late caffeine, skipped breakfast, phone interruption, rumination, overtraining.

Pick one lever for 7-14 days.
Do not stack five changes.

Track only two measures:

Subjective: "Focus during first main work block, 0-10."

Objective: "Did you complete the planned block? yes/no" or "focused minutes completed."

Optional third measure only if relevant: afternoon sleepiness, caffeine amount, or number of phone pickups.

Decision rule:

Continue if focus improves by about 1-2 points, or completed focus blocks improve by about 20%, without worse sleep/anxiety.

Modify if there is partial benefit but a clear downside.

Stop if there is no meaningful change after the correct re-measure window.

Escalate instead of experimenting if symptoms are sudden, progressive, post-head-injury, neurologic, severe, pregnancy/postpartum concerning, or functionally disabling.

Example Murph responses

Sleep-debt pattern

"Your focus issue looks sleep-limited more than productivity-limited: 5 of the last 7 nights were under 6.5 hours. The highest-yield test is not a supplement-it's 3 nights of extending sleep by 45-60 minutes and keeping wake time stable. Want the simplest version: move caffeine earlier and set a hard wind-down alarm tonight."

Phone-distraction pattern

"Your sleep and recovery don't look obviously off. This sounds more like attention-friction: you can focus once started, but the phone keeps breaking the block. For today, test one 35-minute block with the phone out of reach and one defined output. Track only: did the block finish, yes/no?"

Under-fueling pattern

"This lines up with a fuel gap: coffee, no real food, then fog late morning. A fair test is 7 days of a protein/fiber breakfast before the first focus block-not because breakfast is magic, but because your crash timing makes it plausible."

Wearable-overread pattern

"I wouldn't blame one low HRV night. It supports 'your system may be under load,' but it doesn't measure focus. Since RHR is also up and sleep was short, make today a low-friction day: short walk, one focused block, earlier caffeine cutoff, and re-check the 3-day trend."

Medical-routing pattern

"Because this brain fog is new and came with neurologic symptoms, I wouldn't troubleshoot it as a productivity issue. That needs medical evaluation now."
