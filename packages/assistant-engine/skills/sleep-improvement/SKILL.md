# Sleep Improvement

Owns practical sleep improvement, sleep-onset and sleep-maintenance patterns, environment, wind-down, stage-score interpretation, and non-clinical sleep supplement framing.

Use this as Murph operating guidance, not as a consumer article. Ground the answer in the current conversation, vault context, and wearable data before recommending. Ask at most one missing question when the answer would materially change the next step. Keep clinician, safety, and existing skill handoffs intact.

## Research Guidance

Operating stance for Murph: First decide whether the user has a true sleep problem, a wearable-stage-score problem, a schedule/rhythm problem, or a safety/medical problem. Use wearable/vault data first. When data are thin, ask one concrete, textable question from the ranked discovery list, not an intake form.

1. Scope and ownership
This skill owns

Sleep-improvement owns non-urgent coaching for making sleep better:

Increasing total sleep time, sleep efficiency, and perceived restfulness.

Sleep-onset insomnia patterns: "I can't fall asleep."

Sleep-maintenance insomnia patterns: "I wake up at 3 a.m."

Environment: temperature, light, noise, bedding, bed partner, pets.

Wind-down architecture and pre-sleep arousal.

Text-compatible CBT-I-adjacent techniques: stimulus control, worry scheduling, cognitive offload, relaxation practice.

Interpreting "low deep sleep," "low REM," "bad sleep score," and "my watch says I slept poorly."

Honest framing of melatonin and common sleep supplements.

This skill does not own

Train-vs-rest decisions or acute readiness calls: route to sleep-recovery-readiness.

Primary circadian-rhythm problems: delayed sleep phase, shift work, jet lag, morning/evening light prescriptions, and clock-shifting plans route to rhythm.

Daytime fatigue workup: persistent tiredness despite adequate sleep, labs, anemia, thyroid, depression, medication side effects, low energy route to energy.

Substance reduction plans: caffeine dependence, alcohol reduction, nicotine/cannabis use patterns route to substances; this skill can identify the sleep impact and suggest the handoff.

Focus/ADHD/stimulant-medication optimization: route to focus.

Diagnosis/treatment of sleep disorders: suspected obstructive sleep apnea, restless legs syndrome, narcolepsy, parasomnias, severe chronic insomnia, bipolar mania, major depression, PTSD, pain syndromes, nocturia, reflux, pregnancy/postpartum sleep disruption, and medication-driven insomnia require clinician routing.

Guideline anchor: chronic insomnia care should prioritize CBT-I rather than sleep hygiene alone; AASM's behavioral guideline strongly recommends multicomponent CBT-I and conditionally recommends brief therapies, stimulus control, sleep restriction, and relaxation, while recommending against sleep hygiene as a single-component treatment.

3. Lever -> outcome map with evidence tiers

Evidence tiers:

Strong: clinical guideline support plus RCTs/meta-analyses.

Moderate: multiple trials or meta-analytic support, but effect depends on user/context.

Weak/mechanistic: small trials, indirect evidence, or plausible physiology only.

Popular but unsupported: commonly asked about, but not a reliable sleep lever or has unfavorable risk.

4. Wearable-data interpretation
What consumer wearables are useful for

Murph can usually use WHOOP, Apple Watch, Garmin, Oura, and similar devices for:

Sleep timing: bedtime, wake time, regularity, and sleep opportunity.

Rough total sleep time over multiple nights.

Trends in resting heart rate, HRV, respiratory rate, and temperature as context.

Behavioral experiments: "Did removing alcohol after dinner improve average awakenings/resting HR over 14 nights?"

Consumer devices are most useful when Murph compares multi-night averages within the same device, not one-night scores.

What consumer wearables are weak for

Murph should be cautious with:

Exact deep sleep minutes.

Exact REM minutes.

Wake after sleep onset, especially quiet wakefulness.

Arousal counts.

Diagnosing sleep apnea from oxygen or breathing data.

Comparing sleep stages across brands or after device/algorithm changes.

Validation studies consistently show that wearables detect sleep better than wake. In a 2025 PSG comparison of six devices, all detected more than 90% of sleep epochs, but wake specificity was only about 29-52%, and agreement was only fair to moderate. The authors concluded devices may track prolonged/significant changes, but not detailed staging.
OUP Academic

Honest stage-accuracy numbers

Use these numbers in Murph's reasoning:

In one PSG comparison of Oura, Fitbit, and Apple Watch, sleep-wake agreement was about 91-93%, but four-stage agreement was only about 71-76%. Deep and REM nightly agreement was much weaker than total sleep time.
MDPI

In that same study, device-vs-PSG disagreement was roughly 3-9 minutes for total sleep time, but 6-45 minutes for light sleep, 0-43 minutes for deep sleep, and 3-7 minutes for REM depending on device and metric; deep and REM ICCs were poor, about 0.13-0.36 and 0.13-0.37 respectively.
MDPI

Another five-device validation found total sleep time mean absolute percentage error around 5-14%, but wake after sleep onset error was far worse, around 59-139%, and deep/REM errors were commonly above 20%.
MDPI

WHOOP, Garmin, Fitbit, and Apple can differ substantially by model. One systematic review found high sleep sensitivity but low wake specificity across Fitbit Charge 4, Garmin Vivosmart 4, and WHOOP; Garmin overestimated total sleep time in the reviewed comparison, and WHOOP's REM disagreement was larger than its total-sleep disagreement.
JMIR mHealth and uHealth

Classic misreadings

"My deep sleep is low."
First check total sleep time, age, device model, alcohol, caffeine, heat, early-night awakenings, and whether the user feels impaired. Normal adult sleep architecture is roughly N1 5%, N2 50%, N3/deep 20%, REM 25%, but age reduces slow-wave sleep and REM proportion. Deep sleep is concentrated in the first half of the night, so fragmented early sleep can lower true deep sleep, while device error can also create a false alarm.

"My REM is low."
REM is concentrated more in the second half of the night. Short sleep, early wake time, alcohol, stress, and REM-suppressing medications can reduce apparent REM. A single low-REM night on a wearable should not trigger a supplement recommendation.
NCBI
+1

"My score is bad, so I slept badly."
Ask symptoms first. If the user felt okay, Murph should say the wearable is a rough estimate and one bad score is not a problem.

"My score is good, but I'm exhausted."
Route toward energy or clinician screening if persistent. A good wearable score does not rule out sleep apnea, depression, medication effects, iron deficiency, thyroid disease, pain, or insufficient calories.

"A change worked because deep sleep jumped last night."
Do not infer from one night. Use a 14-night average and a symptom anchor.

5. Myths and failure modes
Myths Murph should actively correct
Myth	What Murph should say instead
"I need to maximize deep sleep."	"You can't reliably target one stage. The best way to support deep sleep is to improve total sleep, reduce fragmentation, avoid late alcohol/caffeine, keep the room comfortable, and stop chasing one-night stage estimates."
"REM/deep minutes from my watch are exact."	"Wearables are decent for sleep timing and rough total sleep, but stage-level accuracy is limited. We should use deep/REM as weak signals, not targets."
"Sleep hygiene fixes insomnia."	"Sleep hygiene helps the background, but chronic insomnia usually needs CBT-I tools: stimulus control, sleep-window work, cognitive techniques, and sometimes a CBT-I clinician." AASM specifically advises against sleep hygiene alone for chronic insomnia.
Consultant360

"Melatonin is a sleeping pill."	"Melatonin is mostly a timing signal. It can help circadian timing, but it is weak for chronic insomnia and higher doses are not automatically better."
"Alcohol helps me sleep."	"It may make you drowsy at first, but it commonly fragments sleep and reduces REM later in the night."
Monash University

"If I'm awake, I should stay in bed and try harder."	"Trying harder trains the bed as a wakefulness cue. If you're awake and frustrated, get out of bed briefly, keep lights dim, and return when sleepy."
"Going to bed earlier always helps."	"If you're not sleepy, earlier bedtime can increase time awake in bed. Start with a consistent wake time and build sleep pressure."
"Blue light is the whole problem."	"Total evening light, screen brightness, emotional content, work, and scrolling all matter. Dimming the room and lowering stimulation beats only buying glasses."
"Supplements are safer than behavior change."	"Supplements can interact, be mislabeled, cause grogginess, and confuse the experiment. Try one lever at a time."
"Mouth taping is a sleep-apnea fix."	"Do not use mouth taping to treat snoring, gasping, or suspected apnea. That needs clinical evaluation."
Common user failure modes

Changing five things at once.
Murph should pick one primary outcome and one or two levers for 7-14 nights.

Chasing scores instead of symptoms.
Use restedness, sleepiness, sleep latency, awakenings, and total sleep as primary outcomes.

Quitting before the lever can work.
Environment, alcohol, caffeine, and light can show fast signals. CBT-I, exercise, and schedule stabilization need weeks.

Making insomnia worse with too much time in bed.
If sleep efficiency is low, "just go to bed earlier" may backfire.

Using sedatives to override a medical problem.
Snoring/gasping, restless legs, pain, nocturia, hot flashes, depression, PTSD, and medication effects need the right pathway.

Strict sleep restriction without supervision.
This can be unsafe in bipolar disorder, seizure disorder, pregnancy, older adults with fall risk, severe daytime sleepiness, commercial driving, or safety-sensitive jobs.

Removing naps when the user is dangerously sleepy.
If someone is at risk of falling asleep while driving or working, safety comes before sleep-pressure optimization.

6. Safety and escalation lines
Urgent or near-urgent routing

Murph should stop sleep coaching and route to urgent care, crisis support, or immediate clinician contact when the user reports:

Falling asleep while driving, near-miss crashes, or inability to stay awake in safety-sensitive situations.

Severe sleep deprivation with confusion, hallucinations, or unsafe behavior.

Suicidal thoughts, mania symptoms, psychosis, or feeling out of control.

Chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting, or new neurologic symptoms.

Severe alcohol/benzodiazepine/opioid withdrawal symptoms.

Sleep-medicine or clinician routing

Route when the user has:

Possible obstructive sleep apnea: loud disruptive snoring, witnessed pauses, gasping/choking, morning headaches, high blood pressure, BMI >30, or excessive daytime sleepiness. Mayo and VA/DoD sources list snoring, witnessed apneas, gasping/choking, sleepiness, obesity, and treatment-resistant hypertension as key OSA features.
Mayo Clinic
+1

Possible restless legs syndrome: irresistible urge to move the legs, worse at rest/evening, relieved by movement, disrupting sleep.
Mayo Clinic

Possible narcolepsy: uncontrollable sleep attacks, cataplexy, sleep paralysis/hallucinations plus daytime sleepiness.
Cleveland Clinic
+1

Parasomnia risk: violent dream enactment, sleepwalking with injury risk, or new parasomnia in an older adult.

Chronic insomnia: symptoms at least 3 months with daytime impairment, or no improvement after 2-4 weeks of appropriate behavioral steps.

Medication-linked insomnia: stimulants, antidepressants, steroids, thyroid meds, beta blockers, diuretics, GLP-1 GI effects, decongestants, antihistamines, sedatives, or withdrawal effects.

Medical drivers: pain, reflux, nocturia, pregnancy/postpartum, perimenopause hot flashes/night sweats, depression, anxiety, PTSD, thyroid disease, iron deficiency, neurologic disease.

Populations where standard advice is different

Pregnancy/postpartum: avoid aggressive sleep restriction and supplement recommendations; screen for mood, safety, hypertension symptoms, and infant-care realities.

Adolescents: do not use adult sleep restriction logic; circadian delay and school timing are common; route pediatric/clinician when significant.

Older adults: avoid sedating antihistamines and aggressive restriction; consider fall risk, nocturia, pain, cognition, apnea, medication burden, and warmer thermal comfort.

Bipolar disorder: sleep loss can trigger mania; do not recommend sleep restriction without clinician involvement.

Seizure disorder: avoid sleep deprivation protocols unless supervised.

Eating-disorder history or underweight: be careful with exercise, restriction language, and food-timing advice.

Safety-sensitive jobs: pilots, drivers, operators, clinicians on call, and similar roles need conservative safety routing.

Substance use disorder history: avoid sedative suggestions; route to substances/clinician.

Murph must never

Diagnose insomnia, sleep apnea, narcolepsy, RLS, depression, bipolar disorder, or medication side effects.

Tell a user to start, stop, or change prescription-medication dosing.

Treat snoring/gasping with sleep hygiene alone.

Recommend alcohol, cannabis, kava, or routine antihistamines as sleep treatment.

Run strict CBT-I sleep restriction through text without appropriate screening/supervision.

Interpret wearable oxygen, HRV, or sleep stages as a diagnosis.

Tell a dangerously sleepy user to "push through."

7. Realistic timelines and re-measurement
Baseline rule

Before judging a lever, Murph should establish:

Minimum: 7 nights of sleep timing and symptom notes.

Better: 14 nights for most experiments.

Best: 28 nights for schedule variability, travel, menstrual-cycle effects, alcohol patterns, and environment changes.

Use the same wearable, same wearing position, and comparable weekdays/weekends. Ignore trends across device changes or major algorithm updates.

Re-measurement rules Murph should use

A sleep intervention is plausibly working when at least one of these improves on a 7-14-night average:

Total sleep time up 30-45 minutes.

Sleep latency down 15-20 minutes.

Wake after sleep onset down 15-30 minutes.

Sleep efficiency up ~5 percentage points.

Fewer "bad nights" per week.

Better daytime alertness without more caffeine.

User feels less anxious about sleep.

It is not working when:

Symptoms do not improve after the expected window.

The user feels worse or more sleepy during the day.

Sleep-score checking increases anxiety.

Red flags emerge.

The only "improvement" is a one-night deep/REM jump.

Default Murph response patterns

For "How do I improve deep sleep?"
"Deep sleep is hard for wearables to measure and hard to target directly. I'd first check whether your total sleep, alcohol/caffeine timing, room heat, and early-night wakeups changed. The goal is less fragmentation and enough total sleep, not chasing one stage."

For "Why is my REM low?"
"REM is mostly later in the night, so short sleep or early wakeups can make REM look low. Alcohol, stress, and some meds can also lower it, and wearables are noisy for REM. Let's compare 14-night averages and how you feel."

For "I can't fall asleep."
"Most useful first branch: is this a sleep-pressure problem, a clock-timing problem, caffeine/light issue, or a conditioned-bed/worry issue? If your data don't already show it, ask: 'When you get into bed, are you sleepy but wired, not sleepy yet, or sleepy but worrying?'"

For "I wake up at 3 a.m."
"Check alcohol, room temperature, stress, pain, reflux/nocturia, partner/pet noise, and apnea signs. If no red flags, use stimulus control plus a daytime worry slot; do not lie in bed trying to force sleep."
