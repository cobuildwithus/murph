# Protocol Summary Copy

Last verified: 2026-04-30

## Scope

This governs the protocol frontmatter `summary:` field directly below `title:` on Health Commons protocol pages. That field is the short `/experiments` card description.

The summary should explain the actual protocol in plain language. It is not a research abstract, marketing headline, full safety note, marker list, or metadata repeat.

**Shape:** [Concrete protocol in plain language], [simple mechanism or reason it might matter].

**Rules:**

1. Start with the protocol itself, not an instruction. No "try," "complete," "do," or imperative verbs opening the sentence.
2. Use concrete, plain words. Write so the idea leaps into the reader's head without them noticing the words that got it there.
3. Skip filler like "try," "complete," "prescribed sessions," "routine," "protocol," "intervention," or "experiment" unless genuinely needed.
4. Do not start with duration, frequency, dose window, or session count. Those belong in the protocol spec, not the summary.
5. Do not list marker names as the payoff. No "to see whether resting heart rate, HRV, or sleep efficiency improve."
6. Do not use "to see whether..." as the default ending.
7. Do not make adherence or exposure the outcome. "Repeating the cycle" or "sticking with it" is not the mechanism.
8. Do not make proof claims. The mechanism clause explains *how* it works, not *that* it works.
9. Explain the mechanism when we can do it simply and safely. Go as specific as the document supports — name the physiological process, the substance, the organ response — without overclaiming.
10. If the mechanism would overclaim or the document explicitly says it's unestablished, explain the practical reason for tracking it instead.
11. Keep safety caveats out unless needed to avoid a misleading summary.
12. Remove unnecessary articles ("the," "a") and filler words when cutting them makes the sentence tighter without making it unreadable.
13. Do not say "tracked by phone/wearable" or other measurement-method language. The summary describes the dose and the body's response, not the tracking tool.
14. The mechanism half should speak to how the body reacts, not just what the person does. If it only restates the behavior, push deeper.
15. Keep the whole thing roughly one sentence, about the length of the HBOT example: "Pressurized oxygen chamber treatment, using pressure to carry extra oxygen through the blood to tissue that may not be getting enough."
16. Do not pad for symmetry. If the protocol half or the mechanism half is naturally shorter, let it be shorter.

**Calibration examples:**

- **Finnish dry sauna:** Traditional dry sauna at steady, tolerable heat, where the body opens blood vessels near the skin and the heart pumps harder to move heat out while defending core temperature.
- **HBOT:** Breathing pure oxygen in a pressurized chamber, where the pressure dissolves extra oxygen directly into blood plasma to reach tissue that normal circulation may not supply enough.
- **Norwegian 4×4:** Four 4-minute hard intervals near top aerobic capacity, with easy recoveries between, where sustained high oxygen demand pushes heart to pump more blood per beat and muscles to extract more oxygen.
- **Static stretching:** Gentle held stretches on one target area, where repeated time under mild tension gradually increases how far the joint moves — partly by raising stretch tolerance and partly by nudging tissue to give more.
- **Bryan Johnson sauna:** High-heat dry sauna daily after a workout with groin ice, where stacking intense heat on exercise doubles the demand on heart and blood vessels to move heat out.
- **Caffeine curfew:** All caffeine before late morning or 8 hours before bed, where less residual caffeine near bedtime lets adenosine — the brain's built-up sleep-pressure signal — work without interference.
- **Cold plunge:** Brief head-out immersion in measured cold water, where sudden cold forces a noradrenaline surge and drives blood inward to defend core temperature.
- **Consistent wake time:** Same wake time every day within a narrow window, where a steady morning anchor keeps the body clock from drifting so sleep pressure and circadian timing line up more predictably each night.
- **Daily step floor:** A daily minimum step count, where a visible floor ensures enough steady low-grade cardiovascular and weight-bearing load for the body to adapt to rather than lose.
- **Digital sunset:** No personal screens for the last 30–60 minutes before bed, where removing late light, stimulating content, and notification loops lets arousal drop so the brain can shift toward sleep.
- **High protein intake:** A daily protein floor from real food, where enough amino acids at each meal keeps muscle repair supplied and digestion slow enough to stay full longer.
- **Intermittent fasting (18:6):** All calories inside a 6-hour window each day, where a longer overnight fast lets insulin and blood sugar drop and aligns eating with the hours when the body handles fuel most efficiently.
- **IT band rehab:** Hip and glute strengthening with graded run/walk re-entry, where stronger lateral-hip control reduces the load the iliotibial band puts on the outer knee at each stride.
- **Morning outdoor light:** Outdoor daylight soon after waking, where bright morning light resets the body clock early in the day so sleep pressure and melatonin timing line up better by evening.
